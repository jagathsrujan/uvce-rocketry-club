## 1. WHY RECRUITMENT IS THE CLUB'S PRIMARY RISK

### 1.1 The honest position

The UVCE Rocketry Club begins in 2026–27 with a founder and no committed team. That is the real starting condition, and it should be stated plainly in every planning document: **this Club has zero members until people decide to join, and it will have zero members again in June of whatever year the last committed member graduates.**

Money can be raised in a month. A rocket can be redesigned in a semester. A team cannot be rebuilt quickly, because competent members are made by a year of sustained work, not by a recruitment poster.

The specific failure modes, in order of likelihood:

| Failure mode | What it looks like | When it kills the Club |
|---|---|---|
| **The graduation cliff** | Founding members reach their final year; no juniors have been trained to take over; the Club's knowledge leaves with them | Every May–June |
| **The enthusiasm bubble** | 60 students attend the first demo; 12 come to the second meeting; 4 are still active in October | Weeks 2–6 of the first semester |
| **The founder bottleneck** | Everything runs through one person; when they are busy with exams, the Club stops | Every exam period |
| **The "we'll document it later" spiral** | Nothing is written down; each year's team repeats the previous year's mistakes | Continuously, fatal at handover |
| **The specialised-orphan problem** | One person is the only one who understands the avionics; they graduate or lose interest | Any time |
| **The branch monoculture** | Only mechanical students join; no electronics, no computer science; the avionics and software never mature | Year 2 |
| **The senior-only club** | The Club recruits 3rd and 4th years because they "know more"; they all graduate together | Year 2–3, catastrophically |

**Every one of these is a recruitment and onboarding failure, not a technical failure.** Treat recruitment as engineering: it has requirements, a design, a test campaign (the trial period), metrics, and a review.

### 1.2 How to treat recruitment strategically

1. **Recruit for continuity, not for this year.** The question is never "who can help us now?" It is "who will be here in three years, and what will they know by then?"
2. **Recruit cohorts, not individuals.** A cohort of 8–12 first-years who join together, train together and lead together in their third year is worth more than 30 scattered members. Cohorts keep each other in the Club.
3. **Over-recruit deliberately.** Expect roughly: of 100 students who express interest, 40 attend an event, 25 apply, 15 are selected into probation, 8–10 survive to be active members, 5 become reliable contributors, 2–3 become future leads. **Plan for that attrition and recruit enough at the top to yield 8–10 active members.**
4. **Make recruitment continuous, not seasonal.** One big drive per year plus a low-key rolling intake each semester. A student who discovers the Club in February should be able to join in February.
5. **Measure it.** Track: applications, selection rate, 30/60/90-day retention, probation pass rate, skills matrix coverage, and the number of roles with a trained successor. Review at the monthly Steering Committee. Numbers make the problem visible before it becomes fatal.
6. **Recruit for character and availability, not for prior knowledge.** A committed second-year with no rocket experience beats a distracted final-year with three internships.

### 1.3 The one-line rule

> **Every member's job includes recruiting and training their own replacement. A member who has not trained a successor has not finished their work, however good the rocket is.**

## 2. TARGET PROFILE — WHO TO RECRUIT AND WHY

### 2.1 By year of study — recruit early, without exception

| Year of study | Priority | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1st year (Sem 1–2)** | **HIGHEST** | Four years of runway. They will be present for 3–4 competition cycles. They have the most time, the fewest competing commitments, and the most enthusiasm. They will be competent by the time they are needed to lead. Recruiting them is the single highest-return action available to the Club. They also *become* the institutional memory. |
| **2nd year (Sem 3–4)** | **HIGHEST** | Two to three years of runway; enough maturity to be trusted with real hardware within one semester; typically the ideal cohort to be trained into Leads for the following year. **The 2026–27 founding intake should be weighted toward 1st and 2nd years for exactly this reason.** |
| **3rd year (Sem 5–6)** | MEDIUM | Two years left; they can occupy senior technical roles immediately and often bring skills from internships and other clubs. Recruit them for capability now, but **do not build the Club's succession on them** — they leave in two years, and if the Club is 3rd-year-heavy it has an appointment with the cliff. |
| **4th year (Sem 7–8)** | LOWEST for membership; **HIGH for mentorship** | Their time is dominated by placements, projects and admissions. Recruiting them as ordinary members creates a hole within twelve months. Instead, recruit them as **Alumni Mentors and continuity anchors**, especially anyone who was previously in the Club. |

**Design rule for the founding year:** if the founding Core Team is made entirely of 3rd-semester students (as is likely), then **the Club's stated strategic objective for 2026–27 is not "fly a big rocket". It is "have a trained 1st/2nd-year cohort and a named successor for every role by March 2027."** Everything else is secondary to that, because everything else depends on it.

### 2.2 By branch — build a multidisciplinary Club

A rocket is a systems-engineering artefact and cannot be built by one branch.

| Branch / background | Why they are needed | Subsystems they typically own |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanical Engineering** | Structures, fabrication, thermal, mechanisms, CAD, machining | Structures, propulsion hardware, recovery mechanisms, ground support equipment |
| **Electronics & Communication / Electrical & Electronics** | Avionics, sensors, telemetry, power, PCB, RF | Flight computer, data logging, tracking, ground station |
| **Computer Science / Information Science / AI & ML** | Flight software, data analysis, simulation, tooling, competition documentation systems | Flight software, telemetry decoding, simulation, dashboards, this documentation repo |
| **Chemical Engineering / Chemistry-adjacent** | Propellant fundamentals, thermal analysis, materials, safety data (note: chemistry students must work within exactly the same permitted-materials limits as everyone else) | Propellant characterisation, materials, safety analysis, test-stand instrumentation |
| **Aerospace / Aeronautical (where available)** | Aerodynamics, stability, trajectory | Flight performance, stability analysis, recovery sizing |
| **Civil Engineering** | Structural analysis, site assessment, surveying | Flight dynamics analysis, test-stand structures, range site assessment |
| **Industrial & Production / Manufacturing** | Process planning, quality, inspection, jigs and fixtures | Fabrication planning, inspection, assembly process, BOM discipline |
| **Any branch** — for operations, documentation, finance, design | The Club needs people who can organise, write, draw, photograph, edit, raise money and run events as much as it needs engineers | Operations, documentation, treasury, outreach, media |

**All branches welcome.** Recruit materials should say so explicitly. A rocketry club that is visible only in the mechanical block will be a mechanical club within a year, and will be unable to build avionics.

### 2.3 Behaviours to select for

Look for, in rough order of importance:

1. **Follow-through** — has the person finished something difficult before? A district-level sport, a personal project completed, a paper, an instrument learned, a family responsibility held.
2. **Time availability that is real** — not "I'll manage", but a concrete statement of free slots, checked against their timetable.
3. **Willingness to do unglamorous work** — sanding, tidying, labelling, taking minutes, scanning receipts. Rockets are 10% design and 90% this.
4. **Safety temperament** — accepts instruction, does not treat safety rules as an obstacle to be worked around, does not perform bravado.
5. **Honesty about failure** — admits when they broke something or do not understand something.
6. **Collaboration** — shares credit, helps others without being asked, does not dominate.

Prior rocket knowledge is a bonus, never a requirement. **Aptitude and commitment are trainable-into-skill; expertise without commitment walks away.**

### 2.4 Red flags

- Talks mainly about how impressive the rocket will look.
- Wants a "lead" title in the first month.
- Commits to everything and has committed to five other clubs the same week.
- Disparages safety rules as bureaucratic, or jokes about cutting corners.
- Will not give a phone number or commit to a weekly slot.
- Wants to build "a real missile" or asks about weapons applications. (Politely and firmly decline; this is not the Club's purpose and not something to be associated with.)

## 3. RUNNING AN EFFECTIVE RECRUITMENT DRIVE

### 3.1 The annual campaign calendar

| When | Activity | Owner | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **June–July** | Recruitment plan, poster design, application form finalised, demo rocket prepared | Team Lead + VP | Be ready before the semester starts |
| **July (pre-semester)** | Freshman orientation stall / induction-week presence | All Core Team | First contact with 1st years, before other clubs |
| **Week 1–2 of odd semester** | Poster campaign, social media launch, classroom announcements after lectures (with permission) | Media/Design member + VP | Awareness |
| **Week 3** | **Live demonstration flight** at a permitted site; open to all branches | Operations Lead | Convert interest into intent — the single most effective recruitment event |
| **Week 4** | **Build-a-Rocket workshop** (2 weekends) | Structures + Propulsion Leads | Convert intent into a task done with their own hands |
| **Week 5** | Applications close; short interviews | Team Lead + VP + one Lead | Selection for genuine commitment, not enthusiasm |
| **Week 6** | Selection announced; safety induction; probation begins | Safety Officer + VP | Start the pipeline |
| **Week 8** | Open house / lab tour for latecomers; rolling intake window opens | VP | Catch the second wave |
| **February (even semester)** | Second demonstration flight + mini workshop; rolling intake | Operations + Structures | Continuous recruitment; catches students who missed the first drive |
| **March** | Probation review; confirm new active members; assign role successors | Core Team | Continuity |

### 3.2 Demonstration flights — the highest-conversion recruitment event

Nothing recruits like a rocket that actually flies. But a demo flight is also the Club's highest-risk public activity, and it must be done properly or it will damage the Club permanently.

**Before the demo:**
- Site permission in writing, and any required intimation to local administration, confirmed **well in advance** (see `Institutional_Approvals_and_Compliance.md`). Do not attempt a demo flight on a site that has not been cleared.
- Hazard assessment, written procedure, go/no-go checklist, exclusion zone, fire extinguisher, first-aid kit, and a named Range Safety Officer.
- Flight profile chosen to be **boringly reliable**: a proven low-power commercial motor, a simple well-tested airframe, a large recovery area, calm morning air, a big margin of stability. **Do not debut new hardware at a recruitment demo.** If it fails publicly, you have taught 200 students that this club's rockets do not work.
- A stated weather and wind limit, with a written decision to cancel if exceeded — and someone empowered to make that call on the day.
- Roles: RSO, launch director, pad crew, recovery team, photographer, and a "crowd" person who keeps spectators behind the line and talks to them about the Club.
- Permissions and safety briefing for spectators: where to stand, what they will hear, what not to touch.

**During the demo:**
- Brief the audience for 3 minutes before the countdown: what the rocket is, what the Club does, how to join, and the QR code for the application form.
- Safety brief: stay behind the line, do not approach a landed rocket, follow instructions.
- Countdown and flight. Then — critically — the recovery team retrieves the rocket in front of the crowd, so the audience sees the whole cycle.

**After the demo:**
- Hand out the application QR code immediately, while the crowd is still there. Collect names and phone numbers on a sign-up sheet as a backup.
- Photograph and record the flight for social media (with the flight data), and post within 48 hours.
- Write a one-page demo flight report: motor, impulse class, apogee, recovery, issues, lessons. This is a Test Log entry, not just an event.

### 3.3 The build-a-rocket workshop — the commitment filter

A workshop is the Club's best selection instrument because it tests whether a student will actually turn up twice on a weekend and finish something with their hands.

**Format (two weekends):**

*Weekend 1 (3 hours):*
- 30 min: what the Club does, what members build, what the commitment actually is (be blunt about hours).
- 30 min: safety induction — workshop rules, tool safety, PPE, and the fact that anyone can call a stop.
- 120 min: **every participant builds a simple model rocket kit or a hand-rolled paper/card rocket with a commercially manufactured motor.** Instructor ratio of at least 1:5. All tools demonstrated before use.

*Weekend 2 (3 hours):*
- 60 min: OpenRocket basics — simulate the rocket they built; compare predicted apogee with what they saw (or will see).
- 60 min: launch their rockets (with permissions and RSO in place) or, if a launch is not possible, a static display and a walkthrough of the Club's own vehicle.
- 60 min: how to join; what the probation period involves; question time with the Leads.

**Non-negotiables:** participants sign a safety undertaking before touching a tool; PPE is mandatory; the Safety Officer or a trained deputy runs the safety segment; no participant handles an energetic material of any kind — the Club uses commercially manufactured motors only for public workshops.

**What the workshop gives the Club:** a shortlist of people who have already demonstrated attendance, tool discipline and follow-through, which is far better selection data than an interview.

### 3.4 Posters — practical guidance

- **One idea per poster.** The image is a rocket; the words are five: club name, "Build. Test. Fly.", a date, and a QR code.
- Postcard/A3 size, high contrast, readable from 3 metres. Test it by walking away and seeing if you can still read the date.
- **QR code to the application form** — not to an Instagram page. Every intermediate step loses people.
- Put the same poster everywhere: mechanical, ECE, CS, civil, chemical, library notice board, canteen, hostel notice boards, first-year classrooms, and the department notice boards (with permission).
- A short URL and a WhatsApp group link are more effective in India than an email address. Assume nobody will check email.
- Include a photograph of **the Club's own hardware** if it exists, and honestly label it as a training/test vehicle if that is what it is. Do not use images of other organisations' rockets without permission — that is both an ethics problem (Charter Clause 9.2a) and a credibility risk.

### 3.5 Social media and word of mouth

- **Instagram is the primary channel** for Indian college recruitment; a YouTube channel for flight videos; LinkedIn for sponsors and alumni, not for students.
- Posting cadence: 2–3 posts/week during recruitment season, then at least fortnightly. Content that works: build progress photographs, a real flight video with data overlay, a "what we got wrong this week" post, member spotlights, and a call for a specific skill ("we need someone who can write Python for telemetry decoding").
- **Show the mess, not just the polish.** Sanding, wiring, failed tests and burnt parts recruit better than a rendered rocket, because they signal that a real person can do this.
- **Word of mouth is the strongest channel.** A member who brings one friend is worth ten posters. Give members a reason to bring friends (workshop priority, a small incentive, or simply being asked), and ask explicitly at every general meeting.
- **Classroom announcements** are extremely effective for 1st and 2nd years: ask a friendly faculty member for 3 minutes at the start or end of a lecture, in each branch, in the first two weeks. Do not overrun the three minutes.
- **WhatsApp groups** are how Indian student communication actually happens. Maintain one announcement group (broadcast only, admins = Core Team) and separate working groups per subsystem.

### 3.6 Recruitment drive checklist

- [ ] Recruitment plan written, with dates, owners and target numbers
- [ ] Posters designed, printed, and posted across all branch notice boards (with permission)
- [ ] Social media accounts created and first three posts scheduled
- [ ] Classroom announcement slots requested from faculty, all branches
- [ ] Orientation-week stall approved and staffed by rota
- [ ] Application form live (Google Form or equivalent) with QR code on every poster
- [ ] Demo flight site permission and intimations confirmed
- [ ] Demo flight hazard assessment, procedure, checklist, RSO named
- [ ] Workshop materials, tools and instructor ratio planned
- [ ] Safety undertaking printed for workshop participants
- [ ] Interview panel and scoring sheet prepared

- [ ] Last year's exit-interview notes read, and this year's drive designed to avoid the same problems

## 4. APPLICATION FORM STRUCTURE

Design principle: **enthusiasm is cheap and easy to fake. Commitment leaves traces.** Ask for evidence of past follow-through, for specific available time, and for a small piece of work that costs real effort to produce. Long forms full of "Why do you love rockets?" select for people who write well, not for people who turn up.

Keep it to 12–15 questions and under 10 minutes for a genuine applicant to complete.

### Section A — Basics (2 min)
1. Full name · 2. Institute ID / USN · 3. Branch · 4. Current semester · 5. Expected year of graduation · 6. Phone (WhatsApp) · 7. College email · 8. Hostel or day-scholar (relevant for scheduling and safety).

### Section B — Availability (1 min) — *the single most predictive section*
9. **List the days and time blocks you are genuinely free each week** (e.g. "Mon/Wed 4–6 pm, Sat 10 am–2 pm"). Be realistic: we plan around this, and members who overstate availability and then vanish cost the Club tests and launches.
10. How many hours per week can you commit **in a normal week**, and how many **in a test or launch week**?
11. Are you in any other club, society, sport or job? Which, and how many hours does it take?

### Section C — Evidence of follow-through (3 min)
12. **Describe one thing you have stuck with for six months or more, and one thing you finished that was hard.** (Not necessarily technical. A sport, an instrument, a hobby project, a family responsibility, a competition.)
13. **Describe something you built, made, fixed, coded, wrote or organised.** Include a photograph or a link if you have one — *and tell us what went wrong with it*. Students who can name a failure are the ones who will report one on the range.
14. What tools, software or machines can you already use? (FreeCAD, SolidWorks, Fusion, Arduino, Python, C, a lathe, a drill press, a soldering iron, a sewing machine, a 3D printer, video editing, accounting, design…) Be honest — "I have never used a soldering iron" is a perfectly good answer and will not count against you.

### Section D — Motivation and understanding (2 min)
15. **What do you want to be able to do in two years that you cannot do now?** Be specific to the Club's work.
16. Which of these areas interests you most, in order? Structures/fabrication · Propulsion · Avionics/software · Recovery · Operations and range · Documentation and design · Finance and sponsorship. (Note: stating a preference does not guarantee that role; everyone does some of everything in the first semester.)
17. **The Club's rules mean: no copying other teams' designs or code, no paying anyone to do our engineering, no restricted or hazardous chemicals, and anyone can halt an unsafe operation at any time. Is there anything about these rules you would find difficult?** (This question is deliberate. A defensive or evasive answer is informative.)
18. Are you willing to attend a **two-weekend workshop, a safety induction, and one field test day** within your first two months? (Yes/No plus explanation if No.)

### Section E — Selection task (costs real effort, worth 10 minutes)
19. Choose **one** of the following and submit it with your application:
  - (a) A one-page sketch or CAD model of a model rocket, labelled, with a sentence on why you chose the fin shape.
  - (b) A short Python or spreadsheet calculation: given a rocket's mass, motor thrust curve and drag coefficient, estimate apogee. Show your method; a wrong answer with clear reasoning beats a copied right answer.
  - (c) A 200-word written explanation, in your own words, of how a solid rocket motor produces thrust.
  - (d) A photograph of something you built or repaired, with 150 words on the hardest part.

**Why (d) exists:** it allows a first-year with no technical background, but with real hands and real follow-through, to score highly. That student is often the Club's best long-term member.

### Section F — Consent
20. Consent to be contacted on WhatsApp; acknowledgement of the safety rules; acknowledgement that the first semester is a probation period; and (if under 18) a parent/guardian contact.

### 4.1 Interview (15 minutes, only for shortlisted applicants)

Three people: Team Lead, Vice President, and one Lead. Same questions to everyone, scored on a sheet.

- Walk me through your selection task. What was the hardest part?
- What will you do when you have exams and the Club has a test in the same week?
- You are handed a tool you have never used and asked to cut a part that afternoon. What do you do?
- Tell me about a time you were wrong about something technical.
- Someone senior tells you to proceed with a test you think is unsafe. What do you say, and to whom? *(There is a correct answer: you say stop, and you are entitled to. Anyone who says "I'd do what they say" needs the safety induction before anything else.)*
- The Club meets every week and you have a lab at that time. What happens?

**Scoring:** commitment/availability 30%, evidence of follow-through 30%, safety temperament 25%, collaboration 15%. Anyone scoring zero on safety temperament is not admitted, whatever else they scored.

## 5. TRIAL PERIOD / PROBATION MODEL

**Duration:** one full semester (roughly 12–14 weeks). **Status:** Probationary Member. **No vote; full access to training, meetings and workshop under supervision.**

The purpose of probation is not to make joining hard. It is to make joining *mean something*, and to let both sides find out whether this is a good fit before either invests a year.

### 5.1 Milestones

| Milestone | Timeline | Requirement | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **M1 — Induction complete** | Week 1–2 | Attend safety induction; sign the safety and conduct undertaking; attend one general meeting | Signed undertaking in the file; attendance record |
| **M2 — First hands-on task** | Week 4 | Complete one small fabrication or electronics task under supervision (e.g. build a fin jig, solder a practice board, cut and sand a body tube, log a test) | Task marked done by the supervising Lead; entry in the member's log |
| **M3 — Simulation competence** | Week 6 | Build, simulate and correctly interpret a model rocket in OpenRocket: mass, CG/CP, stability margin, predicted apogee — and explain why the numbers are what they are | Reviewed by Structures Lead; screenshot and one page of reasoning filed |
| **M4 — Field participation** | Week 8 | Attend one full test or launch day in a defined role (pad crew, recovery, logging, photography, safety assistant) | RSO or Operations Lead's sign-off |
| **M5 — Documentation contribution** | Week 10 | Write one real document: a test log entry, a section of the design record, a procurement comparison, a workshop plan, or a short analysis | Accepted into the archive by the Documentation Lead |
| **M6 — Assigned subsystem** | Week 12 | Choose (or be assigned) a subsystem and a mentor; agree the semester deliverable in writing | Recorded in the skills matrix and Succession Register as "in training" |
| **M7 — Probation review** | Week 13–14 | Interview with the Core Team; review the log; decide | Outcome recorded: confirmed / extended by one semester / not confirmed |

### 5.2 Passing probation

Requirements, assessed holistically, not by counting attendance:

1. **Attended at least 70% of general meetings** (with apologies being acceptable and recorded).
2. **Completed at least two of M2–M5 to a standard the supervising Lead accepts.**
3. **Safety record clean** — no unapproved hazardous work, no concealed incident.
4. **Showed up when they said they would.** Reliability is the trait that matters most and the one that is hardest to fake over a semester.
5. **Some evidence of intrinsic interest** — asked questions, read something on their own, proposed an idea, helped someone else without being asked.

**Not confirmed** is not a punishment, and should be communicated kindly and clearly, with a written reason. A person who cannot attend because of timetable clashes may be offered an extension, a deferred entry the following semester, or a lighter involvement (events, outreach, media) where they can genuinely contribute.

### 5.3 Benefits at each stage

| Stage | Gets | Does not get |
|---|---|---|
| Applicant / workshop attendee | Workshops, demos, social events | Workshop access, range access, vote |
| Probationary Member | Training, meetings, supervised workshop and range access, materials for their tasks, a mentor | Unsupervised hazardous work, vote, office, budget authority |
| Active General Member | Independent workshop access, range participation, a vote, eligibility for office, competition team eligibility, a service certificate | Budget authority (unless a Lead) |
| Core Team Member | As above, plus budget authority within limits, leadership, priority for competition roles, letters of recommendation | Exemption from safety rules or documentation duties |

## 6. ONBOARDING CHECKLIST — FIRST 30 / 60 / 90 DAYS

The first 90 days decide whether a member stays for three years. Onboarding is a designed process with named owners, not a welcome message.

### Days 1–30 — Belong, understand, be safe

| # | Task | Owner | Done |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Welcome message with: who's who, the club's WhatsApp groups, the meeting schedule, and where to find `Club_Charter.md` and `Roles_and_Responsibilities.md` | VP | ☐ |
| 2 | **Safety induction** (2 hours): workshop rules, tool safety, PPE, hazardous materials policy, emergency procedures, the absolute right to call a stop, and what happens when someone does | Safety Officer | ☐ |
| 3 | Sign the safety and conduct undertaking (Charter Annexure A) | Safety Officer | ☐ |
| 4 | **Buddy/mentor assigned** and introduced — a 2nd/3rd-year member, not a Lead who is too busy | VP | ☐ |
| 5 | Add to the member register and the skills matrix (with a baseline self-assessment) | Doc Lead | ☐ |
| 6 | Repository access granted; walkthrough of the archive's structure; shown the Design Record and one Test Log | Doc Lead | ☐ |
| 7 | Tour of the workshop and lab; shown where tools, PPE, first aid and fire extinguishers live | Structures Lead | ☐ |
| 8 | Attend two general meetings; introduced to the whole Club at the second | VP | ☐ |
| 9 | Read and confirm: the Constitution's Article 4 (Safety), Article 8 (Succession), Article 9.2 (Ethics) | Member | ☐ |
| 10 | Do the **Name and Story** round at a meeting: 60 seconds on who they are and why they joined (this is not optional for the Club; it is how a cohort becomes a group) | VP | ☐ |
| 11 | First hands-on task (M2) assigned, with a named supervisor and a date | Relevant Lead | ☐ |
| 12 | 30-day check-in conversation, in person, with the buddy and the VP separately | VP | ☐ |

**By Day 30 the member should be able to say:** what the Club is building this semester; who the Leads are; what the safety rules are and that they can halt work; where the files live; and what they personally are working on.

### Days 31–60 — Learn a tool, contribute something real

| # | Task | Owner | Done |
|---|---|---|---|
| 13 | Complete the **OpenRocket pathway** (M3): simulate, interpret, explain | Structures Lead | ☐ |
| 14 | Complete a basic skills module: hand tools and measurement *(mandatory)*, plus one of: soldering, CAD, spreadsheet/data analysis | Relevant Lead | ☐ |
| 15 | Attend a full test or launch day in a defined role (M4), briefed beforehand and debriefed after | Ops Lead | ☐ |
| 16 | Write the first documentation contribution (M5) — a test log, a design record entry, or a comparison of options | Doc Lead | ☐ |
| 17 | Attend a design review as an observer, and be asked for one question or observation | Team Lead | ☐ |
| 18 | Subscribe to the Club's technical reading habit: one short paper, article, or public rocketry document per fortnight, discussed at a meeting | Propulsion/Structures Lead | ☐ |
| 19 | 60-day check-in: what is working, what is confusing, what they want to learn next; record in the skills matrix | VP | ☐ |

**By Day 60 the member should have:** made something with their own hands, run a simulation, stood on a range in a real role, and written something that is in the archive. That sequence is what converts interest into identity.

### Days 61–90 — Own a piece of the work

| # | Task | Owner | Done |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | Subsystem assignment confirmed (M6), with a written semester deliverable and a mentor | Relevant Lead | ☐ |
| 21 | Entered on the **Succession Register** as "in training" for a named role | Doc Lead | ☐ |
| 22 | Present a 5-minute update at a general meeting on their own work | VP | ☐ |
| 23 | Probation review (M7); outcome recorded and communicated in writing | Core Team | ☐ |
| 24 | Given a real, small responsibility with a deadline that others depend on. **This is the moment a member becomes a member** — the Club must give new people real work, not busywork, within 90 days | Team Lead | ☐ |
| 25 | Asked explicitly: "What would make you stay next semester?" Record the answer and act on what is actionable | VP | ☐ |
| 26 | Skills matrix updated with demonstrated competencies (not self-reported only) | Doc Lead | ☐ |

**Warning sign to watch for:** a member who is still only attending meetings at Day 90 has not been onboarded. They have been spectators, and spectators leave. **Give people real work early, with a real deadline, and accept that they may do it imperfectly.**

## 7. SKILLS DEVELOPMENT PATHWAYS

### 7.1 First semester — the core pathway (mandatory for all new members)

| Module | Content | Duration | Assessed by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S1 — Safety induction** | Workshop and tool safety; PPE; hazardous materials policy; emergency procedures; propellant handling limits; the right to halt work | 2 hrs + repeat annually | Safety Officer |
| **S2 — Basic hand tools and measurement** | Drill, hacksaw, files, sanding, taps and dies, vernier caliper, micrometer, scale, square, torque discipline, work-holding | 4 hrs practical | Structures Lead |
| **S3 — OpenRocket** | Vehicle modelling, mass and CG, stability margin (calibers), motor selection, apogee prediction, interpreting and distrusting simulation output | 4 hrs + one simulation report | Structures Lead |
| **S4 — Electronics basics** | Breadboarding, multimeter, soldering to a reliable standard, batteries and safe charging, continuity checks, reading a schematic and a pinout | 6 hrs practical | Avionics Lead |
| **S5 — Documentation** | Naming conventions, the design record, writing a test log, Git basics (clone, commit, branch, pull request), where the archive lives | 3 hrs | Documentation Lead |
| **S6 — Range and field discipline** | Range layout, exclusion zones, roles on the day, countdown discipline, recovery procedure, what to do when something goes wrong | 3 hrs + one field day | Operations Lead + Safety Officer |

**Optional first-semester electives:** CAD (FreeCAD/Onshape/SolidWorks) · Python for data analysis and plotting · composite layup and adhesives · parachute sewing and packing · photography and video editing · technical writing · sponsorship and budgeting.

### 7.2 Progression map

| Semester in Club | Typical capability | Typical role |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | Safety inducted; hand tools; OpenRocket; basic soldering; documentation basics | Probationary → General Member; task-level contributor |
| 2nd | Owns a small subsystem task; can run a bench test; can write a test log unaided | Task owner; competition team member |
| 3rd | Owns a subsystem; can design and defend it at a review; can run a test end-to-end | Sub-team member / deputy to a Lead; project owner |
| 4th | Leads a subsystem; runs reviews; trains juniors; manages a budget line | Lead; competition subsystem owner |
| 5th–6th | Leads a function; runs campaigns; manages people and risk | Core Team: Team Lead / VP / Safety Officer / Treasurer / Doc Lead |
| 7th–8th | Advises, reviews, opens doors, funds | Alumni Mentor |

**Note the deliberate inversion:** a student who joins in the 1st year and stays reaches a Lead role in their 3rd year and a Core Team role in their 4th — with two further years still in hand. That is what a healthy pipeline looks like. **A Club where 3rd-semester students hold every Core Team role has no pipeline at all; it has a queue for the exit.**

### 7.3 Training delivery principles

- **Teach by doing, in pairs.** Every training session produces something physical or a file in the repository.
- **The person who taught it last year should not be the only one who can teach it.** Every module has two named instructors, and the syllabus is written down in the archive with the slides.
- **Record the training.** Every session has a dated agenda and an attendance sheet; every module has a short assessment; failures are re-taught, not failed out.
- **Cross-train deliberately.** At least one member of each subsystem should be able to do a basic version of the neighbouring subsystem's work, so that a single graduation does not create a hole.
- **Train the next instructor.** The demonstrated test of competence for any senior member is: can you teach this to a 1st-year and have them do it unaided?

## 8. MENTORSHIP PAIRING SYSTEM

### 8.1 Structure

- **Every new member gets a mentor within 7 days of joining**, before they have time to drift.
- **Mentors are 2nd/3rd-year active members, not Leads.** Leads are busy and often intimidating; a peer two years ahead is the right relationship.
- **Pairing:** one mentor to one or two mentees, matched by branch where useful but never exclusively — a mechanical mentee paired with an avionics mentor learns more.
- **Duration:** one semester formally, renewable informally. At the end of the semester the mentee changes mentor deliberately, so they learn from two people rather than one.
- **Mentors receive a short briefing** (1 hour) on what the role is: weekly check-in, accompanying the mentee to their first workshop session and first field day, ensuring their questions are answered, and flagging problems to the VP early.

### 8.2 What a mentor does

| Does | Does not |
|---|---|
| Meets the mentee weekly for 15 minutes, in person | Make design decisions for the mentee |
| Sits with them at their first hands-on task | Do the task for them |
| Walks them through the repository and the archive | Assume they will find things on their own |
| Introduces them to other members by name | Leave them standing alone at meetings |
| Answers questions, or finds the person who can | Pretend to know |
| Tells the VP if the mentee is drifting, struggling academically, or unhappy | Keep it confidential when the Club should act |
| Teaches them to report a failure honestly | Protect them from consequences of hiding one |
| Works alongside them at a test or on the range | Let them run a hazardous operation unsupervised |

### 8.3 Mentor accountability

- Mentor pairing is recorded in the member register with dates.
- Mentor effectiveness is reviewed at the 30/60/90-day check-ins: the question asked of the mentee is "has your mentor been useful? What is missing?"
- **Being a mentor is a prerequisite for a Lead role.** No member becomes a Lead without having mentored at least one new member through a full semester. This is a deliberate rule: it makes training the next generation a condition of advancement rather than an optional kindness.

### 8.4 Three-tier mentorship (the full picture)

| Tier | Relationship | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Buddy mentor** | 2nd/3rd-year → 1st-year | Day-to-day support, belonging, basic skills |
| **Technical mentor** | Lead → subsystem trainee | Deep skill transfer in a specific domain |
| **Alumni mentor** | Graduate/industry → Club | Perspective, review, network, funding — within the boundaries in `Roles_and_Responsibilities.md` (no design recommendations) |

## 9. RETENTION — WHY MEMBERS LEAVE AND WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT

Recruiting 12 members and retaining 4 is a worse outcome than recruiting 8 and retaining 7. **Retention is cheaper than recruitment and is the metric that actually predicts survival.**

| # | Reason they leave | Early warning sign | What the Club must do |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Nothing meaningful to do** — they attend meetings for two months and never touch hardware | Stops speaking at meetings; only attends occasionally | Give a real task with a real deadline within 30 days, even if small. "Nothing to do" is the Club's failure, not theirs. |
| 2 | **No sense of belonging** — they never become part of the group | Stands at the edge; no one knows their name | Buddy mentor; name-and-story round; make introductions explicitly; social events; a subgroup small enough to matter |
| 3 | **Exams and academic pressure** | Disappears for 3 weeks in the exam window and does not return | Plan the calendar around exam periods; explicitly tell members to prioritise their degree; run "light weeks"; check in on the other side of exams |
| 4 | **They were never trusted with real work** — seniority gatekeeping | Asks to do something and is told "maybe next semester" | Let juniors do real things early, supervised. Accept imperfect results as the cost of learning. |
| 5 | **Boredom with the pace** — waiting for hardware that takes months | Asks "when do we actually fly?" repeatedly | Keep short-cycle activities running: workshops, small model flights, bench tests, simulations, so there is a visible win every few weeks |
| 6 | **Interpersonal conflict or clique culture** | A member goes quiet after a specific meeting | VP mediates early; enforce the code of conduct; break up cliques by reassigning teams; never leave a member isolated |
| 7 | **Exclusion by language or in-group jokes** | Some members speak only in one language during technical discussion | English as the working language for technical business; explicitly include; rotate teams |
| 8 | **Safety scare or a bad incident** | Members visibly shaken after a test | Blameless incident review; explain what changed; never dismiss a fear; have the Safety Officer talk it through |
| 9 | **Financial pressure** — the Club feels like it costs money | Asks whether trips are paid for | Cover range-trip costs from Club funds where possible; use free transport options; ensure nobody misses a test for want of ₹200; keep a contingency line in the budget |
| 10 | **A better opportunity elsewhere** (internship, GATE, placement, another club) | Mentions interviews or other commitments | Not always preventable. Make the Club's value explicit: real hardware experience, a portfolio, a reference, competition credentials. Keep alumni ties so they come back as mentors. |
| 11 | **No visible progress** — the project keeps stalling | Cynicism in meetings | Publish a plan with milestones and hit them; celebrate small wins publicly; cut scope rather than drift |
| 12 | **The Club is one person's show** | Everything waits for the founder | Delegate authority genuinely; the two-deep rule; let others own tasks and their mistakes |
| 13 | **A failure they were blamed for** | Withdraws after a test failure | Blameless failure culture, stated repeatedly and modelled by the Leads |
| 14 | **Burnout** — 20 hours a week for months | Fatigue, missed meetings, irritability | The VP's workload watch; enforce rest; redistribute; the Club should never ask for more than a member can give |

### 9.1 Retention practices to run every semester

- **The 30/60/90 check-ins**, done properly, with the answers written down and acted on.
- **The "what would make you stay?" question**, asked of every member, once a semester, at a general meeting, anonymously if that helps.
- **Exit interviews for everyone who leaves**, including people who drift away quietly — those are the most informative exits of all. Ask what the Club did wrong; record it in the archive.
- **A win every month**, small and visible: a completed test, a working piece of code, a successful flight, a poster, a photo of a finished part.
- **Public credit.** Name contributors in reports, posts and meetings. Members leave clubs where their work is invisible.
- **A real social life.** One non-technical activity a month. Members stay for the people as much as the rocket.
- **A stated and honoured exam policy.** No Club deadline ever outranks a member's examination. Say it, and demonstrate it once, and people will believe it.

## 10. BUILDING THE PIPELINE — A TRAINED COHORT EVERY YEAR

### 10.1 The four-cohort model

The Club should maintain four overlapping cohorts at all times:

| Cohort | Composition | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Trainees** | 1st years, first semester | Learning fundamentals; task-level contributors |
| **B — Contributors** | 1st/2nd years, second to fourth semester | Owning subsystem tasks; competition team; deputies |
| **C — Leads** | 3rd years | Owning subsystems; running reviews; training Cohort A |
| **D — Core Team / Mentors** | 4th years + alumni | Leading the Club; external relations; institutional memory |

**Each year, every cohort moves up one level, and a new Cohort A is recruited.** If any cohort is empty, the Club has a hole that will open in one to three years. **The Steering Committee shall review cohort sizes at every March Continuity Review and treat an empty Cohort A as an emergency.**

### 10.2 Recruit for the hole, not for the need

Most clubs recruit whoever is enthusiastic. Instead: at every March review, list the roles and competencies the Club will need in **two years**, compare with who will still be present, and recruit specifically for the gap. Examples:

- If everyone in avionics is in the 4th year → next year's recruitment must explicitly target 1st-year ECE/CS students for avionics, and the material must say so.
- If the Club has no one who can write, and competition documentation is 40% of the score → recruit an English/management-adjacent student, or a CS student who writes well, and give them the documentation role for two years so they are competent when it matters.

### 10.3 The pipeline mechanics

1. **Succession Register** (Charter Article 8.1) — a named successor for every role, entered within 30 days of appointment.
2. **Critical Knowledge Register** — every critical subsystem needs two competent people, or it is flagged RED.
3. **Skills matrix** — reviewed each semester against the competencies the next 12 months will require (Section 11).
4. **Mentor prerequisite for leadership** — no one leads without having trained someone.
5. **Deputy roles as training** — every Lead's deputy does part of the job, officially.
6. **Elections in April–May, before the graduating batch leaves**, with the incoming Core Team present for the outgoing team's final six weeks.
7. **March Continuity Review** — the mandatory annual check, with the Faculty Advisor.
8. **Competition continuity** — one member of each subsystem must attend the competition and then brief the whole Club in writing on what was learned, so that the following year's team starts from knowledge rather than from zero.

### 10.4 The two-year rule

> **Any decision the Club makes must be defensible on the assumption that everyone currently in the room will have graduated in two years.** If the answer to "how will this work in two years?" is "someone will figure it out", the design is incomplete.

## 12. ONE-PAGE SUMMARY FOR THE FOUNDING TEAM

1. The Club's job in 2026–27 is not to fly the biggest possible rocket. It is to end the year with **a trained cohort of 1st and 2nd years and a named successor for every role**.
2. **Recruit 1st and 2nd years first.** Recruit across mechanical, electronics, CS, chemical and civil. Recruit for follow-through and availability, not for existing knowledge.
3. **Run a demonstration flight and a build-a-rocket workshop.** They convert interest into intent better than any poster.
4. **Ask for evidence of commitment, not enthusiasm.** Application form Section C and the selection task exist for that reason.
5. **Run a one-semester probation with seven concrete milestones.** Make joining mean something.
6. **Onboard deliberately for 90 days.** Give real work with real deadlines by day 90, or the member is already gone.
7. **Pair every new member with a mentor within a week**, and require mentoring before anyone may lead.
8. **Measure retention, not recruitment.** Review the numbers monthly, and act on the reasons members leave, which are usually the Club's fault and usually fixable.
9. **Maintain four cohorts**, and treat an empty first-year cohort as an emergency.
10. **Document everything, always.** The archive is what makes the next generation possible, and it is the only thing in the Club that cannot graduate.
