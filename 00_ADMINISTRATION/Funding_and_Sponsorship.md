## ⚠️ TWO FACTS TO INTERNALISE BEFORE ANYTHING ELSE

**1. The IN-SPACe Model Rocketry India Student Competition provides NO funding support to participating teams.** Entry to the competition does not come with money, materials, motors or travel support. Every rupee the Club spends on competing — registration, materials, motors, avionics, travel, accommodation, documentation, safety equipment — must be raised by the Club itself. **Verify the current year's position in the rulebook, but plan on the assumption of zero external funding.** Any team that budgets on the expectation of competition support will not make it to the launch.

**2. Money is not the Club's binding constraint — people are.** A well-organised club with ₹25,000 flies something real. A disorganised club with ₹3,00,000 buys equipment that sits in a cupboard after the people who understood it graduate. **Read `Recruitment_and_Onboarding.md` alongside this document.** Funding exists to serve the pipeline, not the reverse.

## 1. YEAR-ONE BUDGET TIERS

Three tiers, with honest statements of what each does and — more usefully — does not buy. All figures are in Indian Rupees and are indicative planning estimates for a Bengaluru-based student club in 2026–27. **They must be converted into a real bill of materials with actual vendor quotations before any money is committed.** Prices for motors, composites, electronics and travel vary and change; treat these as planning envelopes, not quotations.

### TIER 1 — LEAN: approximately ₹25,000

**What this tier is:** a credible, safe, genuinely flying first-year club. This tier is not a compromise on quality or safety; it is a compromise on vehicle size and ambition, and it is the tier the Club should assume it has until it has more.

| Line item | Indicative cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial model rocket motors & igniters | ₹6,000 | Low-power, commercially manufactured motors only. Buy from a legitimate supplier with documentation. |
| Airframe materials (body tubes, nose cones, couplers, plywood/balsa fin stock) | ₹4,000 | Enough for 3–5 small vehicles, with spares for the inevitable rebuilds |
| Adhesives, finishes, consumables (epoxy, CA, sandpaper, tape, thread, elastic) | ₹2,500 | |
| Recovery: parachutes, shock cord, wadding, swivels, nomex | ₹2,000 | |
| Basic avionics: 1–2 small flight computers/altimeters, batteries, buzzers, switches | ₹4,500 | One flight logger + one backup altimeter is the priority |
| Tools and workshop consumables (drill bits, files, blades, calipers, PPE) | ₹3,000 | Partly one-time; the College may provide some |
| Safety equipment (fire extinguisher, first-aid kit, eye protection, gloves, LiPo bag) | ₹2,000 | **Not negotiable at any tier.** Cut the rocket, never the safety kit. |
| Travel and logistics for test/launch days | ₹3,000 | Local travel, local transport of hardware; assumes a site reachable in a day |
| Documentation, printing, stationery | ₹1,000 | |
| Contingency (~10%) | ₹2,000 | |
| **Total** | **≈ ₹30,000** | **Trim to ₹25,000 by reducing vehicle count and motors, not by cutting safety or avionics** |

**What Tier 1 buys:**
- 3–5 small model rockets, designed, built, simulated, tested and flown by students.
- Real flight data from a basic altimeter/logger — the beginning of a Test Log worth keeping.
- A complete, honest bill of materials with estimate-versus-actual costs.
- A demonstration flight and a workshop for recruitment.
- Participation in a competition **only if** the competition's registration cost and travel are separately covered, which at this tier is unlikely. **Be realistic: at Tier 1, the Club's target is competence and continuity, not a competition entry.**

**What Tier 1 does NOT buy:**
- Any static propulsion testing programme (test stand, instrumentation, load cell, pressure transducer, data acquisition). This is genuinely out of reach at ₹25,000.
- Custom motor hardware of any kind.
- Composite airframes, filament winding, or machining beyond hand tools.
- Telemetry/GPS tracking (a basic RF tracker might fit; full telemetry will not).
- Competition travel and accommodation for a team.
- Any spare vehicle. Everything is a single unit, and a lost rocket is a lost capability.
- Durable tooling that survives the year.

**Honest assessment:** Tier 1 is a training and recruitment vehicle. **That is a legitimate and valuable year-one outcome — arguably the right one.**

### TIER 2 — WORKING: approximately ₹1,00,000

**What this tier is:** a competitive club. This is the tier at which the Club can credibly enter and complete the IN-SPACe Model Rocketry India Student Competition, with a test programme and proper documentation.

| Line item | Indicative cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Propulsion: commercial high-power motors and reloads | ₹22,000 | The single largest line. Includes spare reloads/grains for at least one failed flight. |
| Motor hardware (reloadable casing, closures, nozzle, seals) | ₹12,000 | A reusable casing is a capital purchase that serves multiple years — **record it as an asset in the ledger** |
| Airframe and structures: composite materials, tubes, couplers, fins, bulkheads, hardware | ₹15,000 | Includes material for one rebuild after a failure |
| Recovery system: dual-deployment parachutes, deployment charges/CO₂, altimeters, shock cord, protectors | ₹10,000 | Dual deployment is what competition-class flights usually require |
| Avionics: flight computer, IMU/baro/GPS, telemetry transmitter/receiver, ground station, batteries, wiring | ₹15,000 | Includes one spare flight computer or at least the critical spares |
| Ground support: launch rail/pad, ignition system, continuity tester, range box, stands | ₹8,000 | Partly reusable; build rather than buy where possible |
| Safety equipment upgrade (PPE sets, extinguishers, first aid, storage, LiPo bags) | ₹5,000 | Scales with the number of people and the hazard level |
| Competition registration, documentation and submission costs | ₹5,000 | Verify the current fee |
| Travel, accommodation and logistics for competition and test campaigns | ₹10,000 | Long-distance travel for a team of 6–8 is the most commonly underestimated line |
| Printing, exhibition materials, banners, outreach | ₹3,000 | |
| Contingency (~10%) | ₹10,000 | **Do not reduce this.** Something always breaks, and it is usually the expensive thing. |
| **Total** | **≈ ₹1,15,000** | **A workable ₹1,00,000 version is achievable by building the launch rail and ground equipment in-house and keeping travel lean** |

**What Tier 2 buys:**
- A competition-capable vehicle with a real design review trail.
- A static test capability if the Club uses commercial reloads and a simple, safe test configuration — **though a full custom static test programme with instrumentation is still a stretch at this tier**.
- Dual-deployment recovery and flight data logging, which is what makes a technical report credible.
- One rebuild after a failure, which is the difference between "we crashed" and "we flew anyway".
- Attendance at the competition with a full team.

**What Tier 2 does NOT buy:**
- A custom propellant development programme: test stand, load cell, pressure transducer, DAQ, remote firing, blast protection and the safety infrastructure are each individually expensive, and the whole programme realistically starts above ₹2,00,000 — **and it is not the first thing a club should spend money on, because it is the highest-risk, highest-skill activity the Club could attempt**.
- CNC machining, composite autoclaves, or professional fabrication.
- A spare avionics suite for every subsystem.
- Multi-year capital investment: at this tier the Club spends on this year's flight, not on permanent assets.
- Salary, stipends or paid help of any kind — **and the Club must never pay anyone to do its engineering** (Constitution Clause 9.2(b)).

### TIER 3 — AMBITIOUS: approximately ₹3,00,000

**What this tier is:** a club with institutional backing, a multi-year plan, and enough capital to build capability rather than just this year's rocket.

| Line item | Indicative cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Propulsion programme: motors, reloads, multiple flight attempts, spares | ₹55,000 | Includes recovery from at least two significant failures |
| A safe, instrumented **static test capability** (test stand fabrication, load cell, pressure transducer, DAQ, remote firing, blast shielding) | ₹45,000 | The major capability jump at this tier. Requires the Safety Officer's full procedure set and a permitted site. |
| Structures: composite materials, tooling, jigs, a proper fin alignment fixture, curing equipment | ₹35,000 | Capital that serves several years if maintained and documented |
| Avionics: flight computers, full telemetry, GPS tracking, ground station, spares, PCB fabrication | ₹35,000 | Two-deep on flight-critical electronics, which is the whole point |
| Recovery systems, multiple configurations, spares | ₹18,000 | |
| Ground support and range equipment (rail, pad, ignition, range box, test stand ancillaries, generators) | ₹25,000 | Mostly reusable capital |
| Safety infrastructure (PPE for the whole team, extinguishers, first-aid, storage cabinet, hazardous-materials inventory, signage) | ₹15,000 | Scales with the ambition; so does the obligation |
| Workshop: tools, measurement instruments, calibration, storage | ₹20,000 | |
| Competition: registration, documentation, travel and accommodation for the full team | ₹25,000 | |
| Outreach: workshops, demonstration flights, school visits, materials | ₹10,000 | Feeds the recruitment pipeline, which is the point |
| Contingency (~10%) | ₹25,000 | |
| **Total** | **≈ ₹3,08,000** | |

**What Tier 3 buys:**
- A genuine multi-year capability: reusable tooling, an instrumented test capability, redundant avionics, and a documented engineering process.
- The capacity to attempt and survive failure — the single most important thing money can buy in a student hardware club.
- A serious competition entry with credible documentation and a real test history.
- The ability to run outreach and recruitment at scale, which protects the pipeline.

**What Tier 3 does NOT buy, and what money cannot fix:**
- **Continuity.** ₹3,00,000 does not stop a club dying in June. Only successors, documentation and a trained cohort do that.
- **Competence.** Expensive hardware in the hands of untrained students is a hazard, not a capability.
- **Permission.** No amount of money substitutes for approvals, a site, or a compliant scope.
- **A bigger impulse class.** The Club's scope limits are set by the framework in `Institutional_Approvals_and_Compliance.md`, not by its bank balance. **A well-funded club is still bound by 40,960 N·s and by the College's approvals.**
- **A custom propellant programme on its own.** That requires skills, a site, licences and institutional approval first, and money last.

**A caution about Tier 3:** the most common failure at this tier is buying capability the Club cannot maintain. A test stand nobody knows how to calibrate, a telemetry system nobody can debug, and a composite curing setup nobody documented are all liabilities disguised as assets. **Every capital purchase at Tier 3 must have a named owner, a maintenance plan, and a successor identified before the purchase is approved.**

## 2. FUNDING SOURCES

### 2.1 College and department student project funds

**What it is.** Most Indian engineering colleges have a student project fund, a technical festival fund, a Student Welfare fund, or a departmental allocation for student projects, competitions and paper presentations. Amounts typically range from a few thousand rupees to ₹50,000 per project, and are usually released against a proposal and, later, a bill of materials with receipts.

**How to access it.**
- Find out who administers it: the Dean (Student Welfare), the Head of Department, the Technical Committee, or the Student Council. **Ask the Faculty Advisor — this is exactly the kind of institutional knowledge the Advisor's oversight role should provide.**
- Apply in the prescribed format, with the proposal, the budget, the timeline and the expected outcome (competition entry, exhibition, publication, demonstration).
- Attach the Faculty Advisor's endorsement and the Club's recognition letter.
- Ask for a specific amount tied to specific line items. Vague requests get vague answers.
- **Ask about the reimbursement mechanism before spending anything.** Most Indian college funds are reimbursement-based: the Club spends first, submits bills, and is paid later. This has a serious cash-flow implication — **the Club needs working capital or a sponsor who pays up front.** Plan for it.
- Submit bills promptly, in the exact format required, with GST details where applicable. Late or malformed claims are the most common reason student clubs do not get their money back.

**Realistic expectation.** ₹10,000–₹40,000 in the first year. **A first-year club with no track record should ask for less and deliver more**, then ask for more next year with evidence.

**What this source is best for.** Materials, components, motors, and competition registration. Institutions are more comfortable funding consumables and participation than capital equipment.

### 2.2 Department funds

**What it is.** Departments often have lab development funds, seminar funds, or a Head of Department's discretionary allocation. Departments also hold equipment (tools, instruments, machines) that can be made available without any money changing hands.

**How to access it.**
- Build a relationship with the Head of Department **before** asking for anything. The Team Lead and Faculty Advisor should brief the HOD early in the year on what the Club is and what it plans.
- Ask for specific things: "workshop access for six trained members on Saturday mornings" is easier to grant than "₹20,000".
- **In-kind support is often easier to obtain than cash and worth more than its face value.** Lab time, machine access, storage, a room, a projector, the department's 3D printer, transport in a college vehicle — all of these reduce the cash the Club needs.
- Offer something back: the Club's documentation, a demonstration for the department's events, a project exhibition entry, a paper with the department's affiliation, and a pathway that makes the department's students more employable.
- If the department funds a specific item, **label it correctly in the ledger and acknowledge the department in the competition report and on any material produced with it.**

**Realistic expectation.** In-kind support is near-certain; cash is ₹5,000–₹25,000.

### 2.3 Alumni donations

**What it is.** Former members of the Club (once there are any) and, more immediately, UVCE alumni working in aerospace, defence, IT and manufacturing. **Alumni are the Club's most under-used resource, and the one most aligned with its interests.**

**Why alumni give.** They remember their own college hardware project and how hard it was to fund. They want the next generation to have what they did not. They want a visible, tangible connection to something impressive. A message from a student explaining precisely what their money bought — with a photograph of the part and the flight — is far more persuasive than any formal appeal.

**How to build it.**
- **Start a register now**, in year one, even though it has one entry. Every graduating member's name, graduation year, contact, current employer, and what they worked on. **This register is a multi-decade asset.**
- **Publish.** A short annual report with photographs, flight data, the bill of materials and the year's achievements, sent to every alumnus. Include an honest section on what failed and what the Club learned.
- **Ask specifically.** "₹5,000 would buy the flight computer for this year's vehicle. Would you like to fund it?" gets a yes far more often than "please donate".
- **Give recognition in proportion.** A named sponsorship of a component, acknowledgement in the report, an invitation to the launch, and a thank-you in writing — all are meaningful and cost the Club nothing.
- **Never treat alumni as cash machines.** The mentorship value of an alumnus who reviews a design or opens an industry door exceeds most cheques. Ask for their time first; the money often follows unprompted.
- **Be transparent about money.** Indian donors, particularly alumni close to the institution, want to know it is going to students and not to an office-bearer's pocket. **A published ledger audit is the Club's most persuasive fundraising document.**

**Realistic expectation.** ₹5,000–₹50,000 in the first year if the Club works at it; growing substantially as the alumni base ages. **Alumni are also the best source of recurring, low-friction annual support** — a small annual appeal to a warm list.

### 2.4 CSR from local aerospace and engineering companies

**What it is.** Corporate Social Responsibility funding under Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013, under which qualifying companies must spend a percentage of profits on specified activities. **Education, skill development and promotion of science and technology are common CSR themes**, and Bengaluru has one of the densest concentrations of aerospace, defence, engineering and IT companies in India. This is the largest single potential source available to the Club — and the hardest to access in year one.

**What CSR decision-makers actually need (and why student clubs usually fail):**
- **A registered, legally identifiable recipient.** Most CSR funds cannot be paid to a group of students or to an individual's bank account. Payment generally must go to a registered entity — commonly the College, a registered society or trust, or a Section 8 company — against a formal proposal and a utilisation certificate. **This is the single biggest structural obstacle. Resolve it with the College before approaching any company.**
- **Documentation**: a proposal, a budget, a timeline, deliverables, an identified implementing agency, and a commitment to report utilisation. Many companies want an 80G/12A status or a registered trust to receive funds; **check what the College can offer and what the company requires.**
- **A named contact inside the company.** Cold emails to `csr@company.com` rarely work. A warm introduction — through an alumnus, a faculty member's research collaboration, a supplier, or a parent — almost always does.
- **Scale.** CSR budgets are often in lakhs and have minimum thresholds and internal approval levels. A ₹15,000 request may be administratively harder to process than a ₹1,50,000 one. **Consider partnering with the College to present the Club's needs as part of a larger institutional ask** — a lab upgrade, a student innovation centre or a skilling programme, within which the Club's rocketry programme sits as a named component and beneficiary.
- **A measurable outcome.** Companies want to report an impact. "60 students trained, 12 flights conducted, 2 competition entries, 1 rural school outreach" is reportable. "We built a rocket" is not.

**Realistic expectation.** Unlikely in year one; achievable in year two or three with the College's active partnership and a track record. **Do not build the year-one budget on CSR.** Start the relationship in year one, ask for something small or in-kind (a plant visit, a mentoring session, a materials donation), and grow it.

**Which companies to approach.** Bengaluru-based aerospace, defence, space and advanced-manufacturing organisations, their suppliers and their engineering services partners, plus large IT companies with education CSR programmes. **Approach via a personal connection; approach in the company's language (skills, employability, STEM education), not the Club's (we need a motor).**

### 2.5 Crowdfunding

**What it is.** Online fundraising through platforms, plus the Indian-specific variants that actually work for student clubs: UPI-based collection from a known network, alumni WhatsApp groups, and a fundraising page shared by members and their families.

**What works in practice:**
- **A specific, costed ask with a deadline.** "₹18,000 by 15 December to buy the flight computer and motors for our competition vehicle. Here is the bill of materials." Vague appeals fail.
- **A video and photographs.** 60–90 seconds of students building and flying something. Faces, hands, workshop, flight. Not a render.
- **A reward structure at low price points.** ₹500 — name on the supporters' page in the technical report; ₹1,000 — Club sticker and T-shirt; ₹5,000 — sponsor a named component and be credited in the report and on the vehicle; ₹25,000 — logo on the airframe and a launch-day invitation.
- **Members sharing it personally**, with a message, not a broadcast link in a group of 500 people.
- **Trust signals**: the College's name and recognition, the Faculty Advisor's name, a published budget, a commitment that funds go to the Club account and are audited, and a published report afterwards.
- **Platform fees matter.** Domestic platforms typically charge a percentage; international platforms may not support Indian payment collection cleanly. **Compare before choosing, and check whether the College can receive the funds directly, which may be simpler and cheaper.**
- **Follow the money with a report.** Send every donor a short report with photographs and a thank-you. It is the single thing that converts a one-time donor into a repeat donor.

**Realistic expectation.** ₹10,000–₹50,000 from a well-run student crowdfunding push, largely from family, friends, alumni and local well-wishers. **The realistic ceiling for a student club is modest; treat crowdfunding as a supplement that also builds awareness, not as a primary source.**

### 2.6 Competition prizes

**What it is.** Prize money, grants or awards from the IN-SPACe Model Rocketry India Student Competition, other student competitions (technical festivals, innovation challenges, hackathons, design contests, national science exhibitions, ISRO/DRDO-adjacent student programmes, IEEE and ASME student competitions).

**What to know:**
- **Prize money is uncertain, delayed, and arrives after the expenses.** It cannot fund the attempt. Treat it as a bonus that funds *next* year.
- **The IN-SPACe competition provides no funding support to participating teams.** If the Club wins a prize, verify the payment route, tax treatment and whether it must be received by the College.
- **Other competitions are a genuine and under-used funding and credibility source.** A well-presented rocketry project often wins innovation challenges and technical exhibitions that have nothing to do with rocketry — and those prizes are frequently more accessible than aerospace-specific funding.
- **Winning prizes is a fundraising tool in itself.** "Winner, [competition] 2027" transforms the next sponsorship proposal and the next college funding application.
- **Record and bank every prize properly**, with the awarding body's letter, and acknowledge it publicly.

**Realistic expectation.** ₹0 in year one if the Club does not compete. Over time, prizes can fund a meaningful fraction of the annual budget — **but only after the Club has a track record.**

## 3. HOW TO WRITE A SPONSORSHIP PROPOSAL

### 3.1 The mindset

A sponsor is not a donor. A sponsor is buying something: visibility, association, a pipeline of future employees, evidence of community contribution, or a good story to tell. **The proposal's job is to make the exchange obvious, specific and easy to approve internally.**

Most student sponsorship proposals fail for four reasons:
1. They ask for a round number ("₹50,000") without saying what it buys.
2. They offer "logo on the rocket" and nothing else, which is worth very little.
3. They are addressed to a generic company rather than a named person with a reason to care.
4. They contain no evidence the team can actually deliver.

### 3.2 Structure of a sponsorship proposal

**Keep the whole thing to 4–6 pages.** Nobody reads more.

| Section | Pages | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Cover** | — | Club name and logo; the College's name; a single striking photograph of the Club's own hardware; the ask in one line ("Sponsorship of ₹25,000 to fly a student-built rocket at the IN-SPACe national competition, 2027"); contact details |
| **2. Who we are** | ½ | The Club in three sentences. Founded when, how many members, which branches, what it has built and flown. The recognition letter reference. The Faculty Advisor's name and designation. **If the Club is new, say so honestly — and say why that makes the sponsorship formative.** |
| **3. What we are doing this year** | 1 | The vehicle, the mission, the target impulse class, the competition, the timeline with milestones. One photograph or a labelled CAD image. Be specific and modest: an overstated project destroys credibility when it slips. |
| **4. Why it matters** | ½ | The impact: how many students trained, the skills developed, the outreach conducted, the competition entry, the credibility it brings the department and the College. **This is the section CSR and institutional sponsors care most about.** |
| **5. What your money buys** | 1 | **The costed bill of materials.** Every line item with a cost. Sponsors want to see that ₹25,000 buys a flight computer, motors and recovery hardware — not vague "project expenses". Include what the Club is contributing itself (member time, college in-kind support, other sponsors), because co-funding proves seriousness. |
| **6. What you get** | 1 | The sponsorship tiers table (Section 3.4), with concrete, deliverable benefits — and a realistic statement of what the Club can and cannot promise. |
| **7. Track record and team** | ½ | Photographs, flight data, prior results, a short list of the team's roles. If year one, use the workshops, simulations and any completed tests. |
| **8. The ask and next steps** | ¼ | Exact amount, exact use, what happens next, and one clear action: "Please confirm by [date] so that we can place the order in time for the March test window." Include a named student contact with a phone number and email, and a note that the Faculty Advisor is available to speak. |
| **Annexures** | as needed | Recognition letter; Faculty Advisor's note; the Club Constitution's safety clause; bank/recipient details; a letter of intent template; photographs |

### 3.3 What sponsors actually want

| What they want | How the Club provides it |
|---|---|
| **Visibility with a specific audience** | Named logo placement on the vehicle, the team's shirts, the ground station, the report and the website; and honest reach numbers (how many students, how many event attendees, social media reach) |
| **Association with something impressive** | A real flight, real data, a competition entry, a photograph of their logo on a rocket at apogee. **This is genuinely valuable and the Club should not undersell it.** |
| **A cost-effective, low-risk transaction** | Small amounts, a clear budget, a written acknowledgement, a utilisation report, no legal complexity. Make it easy. |
| **A named person to deal with** | One student contact who answers the phone and replies within a day. Not a committee. |
| **A story for their own communication** | Offer to write the content for them: photographs, a 200-word blurb, a short video. Companies are short of bandwidth, not of channels. |
| **Talent pipeline** | The club's best students are exactly the interns they want. Offer an **internship pipeline** explicitly — a first look at the Club's active members for internships, projects and hiring. **This is often worth more to a company than the logo, and it costs the Club nothing.** |
| **Technology or capability access** | Access to the team's work, a paper, a joint presentation, a factory visit in return. |
| **CSR compliance and reportability** | A measurable output count, photographs, a utilisation certificate, and prompt reporting. |
| **Recognition within the institution** | Acknowledged to the College and the department, mentioned in the annual report and at any College event. Sponsors value the institutional relationship. |
| **Low effort** | A one-page agreement, no negotiation, no follow-up chasing. **The Club should do all the work.** |

**What sponsors do not want:** vague asks; being handed a "₹1,00,000 or nothing" proposition; being asked for money by a student who cannot explain the project; sponsorship requests that arrive three weeks before the deadline they must fund; and any association with a project that looks unsafe, unapproved or likely to end in a newspaper headline for the wrong reason.

### 3.4 Sponsorship tiers — a template

| Tier | Amount | Benefits |
|---|---|---|
| **Associate** | ₹3,000–₹10,000 | Logo on the Club's supporters' page, website and social media acknowledgement; a thank-you letter; the annual report |
| **Bronze** | ₹10,000 | All of the above, plus logo on the team T-shirt; a named component ("the flight computer sponsored by —"); acknowledgement in the competition technical report |
| **Silver** | ₹25,000 | All of the above, plus logo on the launch vehicle airframe; a launch-day invitation for two representatives; a framed flight photograph with data; a mention in the launch video |
| **Gold** | ₹50,000 | All of the above, plus principal sponsor billing on all team material; the vehicle itself can carry the sponsor's name; a dedicated launch-day demonstration and site visit; an internship-pipeline first-look arrangement; a presentation to the sponsor's team |
| **In-kind** | Value equivalent | Materials, components, motors, machining, transport, printing, software licences, lab access. **Value it and recognise it at the equivalent tier** — in-kind sponsors should be treated exactly as well as cash sponsors. |

**Rules the Club should apply to sponsorship:**
- **Only promise what can be delivered.** An unfulfilled sponsorship promise is a burnt relationship and a reputational loss.
- **No sponsor may influence a design or safety decision** (Constitution Clause 13.8). Do not accept a sponsorship conditional on a design choice, a supplier selection, or a flight decision.
- **Do not put a sponsor's logo where it could imply their endorsement of the activity's safety or legality.** A short clause in the agreement makes this explicit.
- **Keep the amount confidential where asked, but never the existence of the sponsorship.** Public acknowledgement of who supported the Club is both honest and useful for next year.
- **Acknowledge in writing within a week of receipt**, with the amount, the purpose, what was promised and when. **Then actually deliver, and follow up with photographs and the utilisation report.**

### 3.5 Finding the right person

- **Never cold-email a company.** Identify a named individual: a CSR manager, a marketing or brand manager, an HR/talent-acquisition manager, an engineering manager, or the founder of a small aerospace company. Different people buy different things — CSR buys impact, marketing buys visibility, HR buys the pipeline.
- **Route:** alumni → faculty research collaborations → suppliers and vendors the Club already buys from → parents and family networks → the College's industry-relations or placement office → LinkedIn, to a named person, with a specific, short message.
- **Make the first ask small.** A 20-minute meeting, not ₹50,000. Bring the one-page deck, one photograph of the Club's hardware, and one clear question.
- **Follow up exactly when you said you would,** with a thank-you and a status update. Most student clubs never follow up; the ones that do get funded.

### 3.6 One-page sponsorship deck structure (10 slides maximum, if using slides; the one-pager is better for a first contact)

**If one page (recommended for first contact):**

## 4. ALUMNI ENGAGEMENT STRATEGY

### 4.1 Why alumni matter

Alumni are simultaneously the Club's **funding source, its mentorship pipeline, its institutional memory, and its proof that the Club produces something worth joining**. A club with a functioning alumni network has a permanent answer to the graduation cliff; a club without one dies every June.

**The strategic point:** alumni engagement must start in year one, when there is nobody to engage. The Club should build the register, the annual report habit and the relationship **before** it needs anything, because asking an alumnus for money the first time you contact them does not work.

### 4.2 The alumni register (start in year one)

| Field | Notes |
|---|---|
| Full name, graduation year, branch | |
| Roles held in the Club; subsystems worked on | So that requests can be matched to expertise |
| Current employer, designation, city | Builds the industry map |
| Contact: personal email, phone, LinkedIn | Personal email — college emails expire |
| Willing to: mentor / review documents / speak at an event / donate / offer internships / host a visit | Consent-based; ask explicitly |
| Notes: what they said in their exit interview | Their warnings are the next team's syllabus |

**Consent and privacy:** ask permission before adding anyone to a mailing list or a group, and honour requests to be removed. Treat contact details as confidential (Constitution Clause 14.6).

### 4.3 Annual engagement calendar

| When | Activity | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| **March** | Exit interviews with graduating members; invitation to join the alumni register; handover of alumni contact to the incoming Team Lead | Documentation Lead |
| **May/June** | Graduation send-off; a Club memento; a photograph of the graduating cohort with the vehicle they built | Team Lead |
| **July** | Annual report published and sent to every alumnus: achievements, photographs, flight data, the bill of materials, what failed, and what the Club needs next year | Documentation Lead |
| **August** | Alumni appeal — a specific, costed ask with a named component to sponsor | Treasurer / Alumni Mentor |
| **October** | Alumni mentorship evening: online or on campus; alumni speak about industry, careers and rocketry; members get a network | Vice President |
| **December** | Invitation to the test or launch campaign; a demonstration for alumni and their families | Operations Lead |
| **February** | Design review with alumni as reviewers (within the boundaries: **no design recommendations** — see `Roles_and_Responsibilities.md` §11) | Team Lead |
| **March** | Thanks and reporting: what their money bought, with photographs; the utilisation report | Treasurer / Documentation Lead |

### 4.4 What to ask alumni for, in order

1. **Their time and their story** — the exit-interview note, a lessons-learned write-up, a talk. Ask for this first.
2. **Their network** — an introduction to a company, a supplier, a sponsor, a laboratory, a graduate programme.
3. **Their review** — reading a design record or a draft report and asking hard questions (Socratic only).
4. **Internships and opportunities for current members.**
5. **Money** — last, specifically, and with a stated purpose.

**Why this order matters:** an alumnus who has been asked for their story and their advice for two years will give money readily. An alumnus whose first contact in three years is a payment request will not.

### 4.5 Converting goodwill into funds

- **Name a component after the donor's cohort.** "The 2027 batch flight computer" gives a graduating class a concrete, repeatable tradition of giving — and cohorts tend to give collectively.
- **Set up a small recurring appeal.** ₹1,000 a year from thirty alumni is ₹30,000 a year with almost no effort, once the habit exists.
- **Report every rupee.** Publish the ledger summary and photographs of what the money bought. This is what makes year two's appeal succeed.
- **Make it easy to give.** Correct account details, a clear purpose, an immediate written acknowledgement, and no administrative friction. Where the College must receive the funds, explain exactly how and to whom.
- **Recognise publicly and appropriately** — in the report, on the supporters' page, at the launch, in the video.

### 4.6 Alumni as mentors

Beyond money, alumni are the Club's continuity across generations. Their role is bounded deliberately (see `Roles_and_Responsibilities.md` §11): they review, question, connect and encourage — **they do not make design decisions or give design recommendations.** A mentor who designs the rocket creates a dependency that breaks exactly when the Club most needs independence, and it steals the learning that justifies the Club's existence.

### 4.7 What the Club gives alumni

- **Purpose.** A tangible connection to students building something real.
- **Access to talent.** Alumni working in industry get a first look at motivated, technically trained students.
- **Community.** An annual event, a group, a shared identity.
- **Pride and visibility.** Their name attached to a flying vehicle, a competition entry, a report.
- **The satisfaction of preventing the exact problems they faced.** Many alumni will give generously to ensure the next batch does not have to fight for a flight computer the way they did.

## 5. FINANCIAL CONTROLS

**These controls implement `Club_Charter.md` Article 13. They are not bureaucracy: they are what makes the Club fundable. A sponsor, a college fund and an alumnus all want the same assurance — that the money is recorded, approved, spent on what was promised, and auditable.**

### 5.1 Who approves what

| Amount | Approver | Record |
|---|---|---|
| Up to ₹2,000 | Relevant Lead / Operations Lead, within an approved budget line | Ledger entry, receipt filed within 7 days |
| ₹2,001 – ₹10,000 | Treasurer **and** Team Lead, in writing (email or message, filed) | Written approval filed with the ledger entry |
| ₹10,001 – ₹50,000 | Core Team resolution at a quorate meeting, minuted; Faculty Advisor informed | Minute reference recorded against the payment |
| Above ₹50,000 | Steering Committee resolution with the Faculty Advisor's concurrence; College procurement rules complied with | Minute reference and written concurrence filed |
| **Any amount to a related party** (a member, a member's family business, a member's startup) | **Full Core Team resolution, disclosed in the minutes, with the Faculty Advisor informed** — and competitive quotations required | Documented to remove any appearance of impropriety |

**Two rules that do not bend:**
- **Two approvals for anything above ₹2,000.**
- **No Club money in a personal account.** Not a UPI wallet, not a cash box, not "temporarily". The Club's funds stay in the Club's or the College's account (Constitution Clause 13.1).

### 5.2 Receipts

- **No receipt, no reimbursement**, for anything above ₹500. Between ₹0 and ₹500, a written note initialled by the Treasurer and Team Lead, with the reason recorded.
- Receipts collected at the point of purchase — not reconstructed later. Photograph the receipt immediately; thermal paper receipts fade to blank within months, which is a real and common problem in India.
- Every receipt scanned and filed within **7 days**, named consistently (`2026-11-14_motors_reloads_INR8400_receipt_Ravi-Traders.pdf`), and cross-referenced in the ledger.
- Original receipts kept in the physical financial file for the semester, then retained per the records policy (Constitution Article 12.8 — financial records are permanent).
- Invoices must be proper tax invoices where the vendor is registered, with GSTIN and the College's or Club's name as applicable. **Check with the College before purchasing whether the bill needs to be in the College's name for reimbursement** — buying in a student's name is a common and expensive mistake that invalidates a college claim.

### 5.3 Running ledger

Maintained by the Treasurer, updated within 48 hours of any transaction. Template in Section 7.

**Discipline:**
- The ledger is **updated continuously**, not reconstructed at audit time.
- Balance is reconciled against the bank statement **monthly**, with a signed reconciliation note.
- The Treasurer reports income, expenditure and balance at **every General Body Meeting** — a two-minute verbal report is sufficient and keeps everyone informed.
- Every member may inspect the ledger and receipts on request.

### 5.4 Cash handling

Cash transactions shall be avoided wherever possible. Where cash is unavoidable (rural vendors, urgent consumables at a test site):
- The Operations Lead may hold a standing imprest of not more than **₹5,000**, reconciled within **7 days** of each event.
- Two people count and record cash movements on the day.
- A cash voucher, signed by the spender and one witness, accompanies every cash entry.
- Any cash loss, however small, is reported to the Treasurer the same day and recorded. **Do not quietly absorb a shortfall** — an unexplained gap in a student club's books becomes a serious accusation later.

### 5.5 The bill of materials with estimate versus actual — a competition requirement

**The IN-SPACe Model Rocketry India Student Competition requires participating teams to document a complete bill of materials with estimate-versus-actual costs.** This is not optional paperwork; it is part of the submission, and it is also the most useful management document the Club will produce.

- **Owner:** Treasurer, jointly with the Documentation Lead. Each Lead owns their subsystem's rows.
- **Timing:** opened at the start of the project; updated at every purchase; finalised within two weeks of the last flight; filed with the competition submission.
- **Content:** every item, quantity, unit cost, vendor, estimated cost, actual cost, variance, and where the funded money came from.
- **Honesty:** actual means actual. Do not adjust estimates retroactively to flatter the variance. **Record the variance and explain it** — a well-explained 30% overshoot on motors is credible engineering; a suspiciously perfect estimate is not, and an inaccurate BOM in a competition submission is a serious integrity issue (Constitution Clause 9.2(d)).
- **Variance analysis:** anything over ±15% gets a one-line explanation. This is how next year's team learns to estimate.

**Why it matters beyond the competition:** the BOM is how a sponsor's ₹25,000 becomes visible, how next year's budget becomes realistic, and how the Club proves it is not wasting money.

### 5.6 Audit

- **Every semester**, by two people who are not signatories — ordinarily the Documentation Lead and one General Member nominated by the general meeting, or the College's accounts office where it offers this.
- Scope: bank statement against ledger; receipts against entries; approvals against Clause 5.1; BOM against actual expenditure; sponsorship income against the sponsorship register.
- **Report presented to the General Body within 30 days of the semester's end** and filed in the archive.
- Any discrepancy reported to the Team Lead and the Faculty Advisor immediately, with a written explanation and a corrective action. **An honest shortfall reported promptly is a management matter; a concealed one is a disciplinary matter.**

### 5.7 Sponsorship money — specific controls

- Received into the **Club or College account only**, never a personal account.
- **Acknowledged in writing within 7 days**, with the amount, the purpose, and the benefits promised.
- Recorded in the **Sponsorship Register** (Section 7.3) with the deliverables and their completion dates.
- **Spent on what it was raised for.** Where a sponsor has designated a purpose, the ledger's budget line must reflect that designation, and the utilisation report must show it.
- **If the Club cannot deliver a promised benefit, tell the sponsor immediately** and offer an alternative. Silence is what destroys the relationship.
- **No sponsor may influence a design or safety decision** (Constitution Clause 13.8). If a sponsorship is offered on that condition, decline it, politely, and record the declination.
- **A utilisation report to every sponsor** within 30 days of the launch or the project's end: what was spent, what it bought, photographs, flight data, and thanks.

### 5.8 What the Club must never do

- Hold Club money in a personal account or wallet.
- Spend without an approval above the threshold "because it was urgent". **Urgency is a planning failure; it does not create authority.** Where a genuine emergency purchase is unavoidable, obtain the Treasurer's or Team Lead's approval by message at the time, and record it within 24 hours.
- Pay a member, or a member's relative or business, without a full Core Team resolution and disclosure (Section 5.1).
- Reimburse without a receipt above ₹500.
- Promise a sponsor a benefit the Club cannot deliver.
- Adjust a BOM, ledger or report to look better.
- Accept funding with conditions attached to design, safety or supplier choices.

## 6. THE YEAR-ONE FUNDING PLAN — A WORKED EXAMPLE

**Scenario:** founding year 2026–27, a Core Team of five 3rd-semester students, a target of reaching the IN-SPACe competition in the following cycle, and a realistic aim of building a trained cohort of twelve 1st/2nd-year members.

| Target | Amount | Source | Owner | When | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Workshop access, storage, a room, machine time | In-kind | Department / College | Team Lead + Faculty Advisor | Aug | Ask early; costs the department nothing |
| Materials, motors, components | ₹20,000 | Department student project fund | Treasurer | Sep | Formal proposal with budget and timeline |
| Competition registration plus materials | ₹10,000 | College Student Welfare / technical fund | Team Lead | Sep | Recognised club with Faculty Advisor endorsement |
| Safety equipment and PPE | ₹8,000 | College fund (safety framing is persuasive) | Safety Officer | Oct | Tied to a written safety plan |
| Flight computer, motors, recovery | ₹15,000 | Alumni donations (specific, named ask) | Treasurer + Mentor | Aug–Nov | Annual report + named-component appeal |
| General fund | ₹12,000 | Crowdfunding (family, friends, well-wishers) | VP | Oct | Video + costed ask + reward tiers |
| Tools and consumables | ₹5,000 | In-kind donation from a local supplier or workshop | Operations Lead | Nov | Ask a vendor the Club already buys from |
| **Total year-one target** | **≈ ₹70,000 (≈ ₹50,000 cash + in-kind)** | | | | |
| **Planned capability** | **Tier 1–2: flying model rockets, real flight data, one competition attempt if registration and travel are separately funded, a trained first-year cohort, and a complete BOM** | | | | |

**Note the composition.** More than half comes from sources that require no legal complexity: the College, the department, alumni and a small crowdfunding push. **CSR is deliberately not in the year-one plan** — the Club starts that conversation in year one and banks on it in year three.

**Note the sequencing.** Funding work begins in **August** for a test campaign in the following **March**. Money raised in March is money raised too late. **Fundraising is a semester-long activity, not a fortnight's panic.**

## 8. THE FIVE THINGS THAT MATTER MOST

1. **Assume zero competition funding.** The IN-SPACe competition provides no funding support; every rupee is the Club's to raise. Budget accordingly.
2. **Plan the year on Tier 1 money with Tier 2 ambitions, and do not commit to a Tier 3 plan on Tier 1 funding.**
3. **Raise money in the semester before you need it.** Fundraising has the second-longest lead time of anything the Club does, after approvals.
4. **Controls are what make the Club fundable.** Two approvals, receipts, a running ledger, a published audit and an honest BOM are not red tape — they are the reason a sponsor, a college fund or an alumnus will say yes, and say yes again.
5. **Alumni are the Club's compounding asset.** Start the register in year one, ask for their time before their money, and report back on every rupee. A club with a real alumni network has solved both its funding problem and its continuity problem at the same time.
