# Stage 5 — Essay and Assessment

**500 words. One prompt. Read for thinking, not for knowledge.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **When** | Week 3–4, submitted alongside the open-book test |
| **Length** | 400–600 words. Do not pad. |
| **Time** | 45–60 minutes |
| **Marked out of** | 20 |

---

## Why an essay after a test

The test measures whether someone read a document. **That is a weak proxy for what we actually want.**

The essay catches what the test misses:

| The test reveals | The essay reveals |
|---|---|
| Did they read it? | Did they *think* about it? |
| Can they find information? | Can they form an opinion and defend it? |
| Can they apply a formula? | Do they understand what they are getting into? |

> **Some of our best future members will score average on the test and excellent here.** That is the
> entire reason this stage exists.

---

## The prompts

**Choose ONE.** All are equally valid. There is no preferred answer.

---

### Prompt A — The honest one

> **"What do you think will be the hardest part of this for you personally, and why?"**

**Why we ask it:** self-awareness. We are not looking for confidence. A candidate who says *"I am
worried I will lose interest when things fail repeatedly, because that has happened to me before"* is
more useful to us than one who says *"nothing will be hard, I am very dedicated."* The first person
knows their own failure mode.

---

### Prompt B — The thinking one

> **"The Foundation Reader says a stronger motor casing is not automatically safer. Explain this idea
> in your own words, and describe one other situation in engineering or everyday life where 'more
> strength' or 'more safety margin' could actually make things worse."**

**Why we ask it:** whether they can take a counterintuitive principle and transfer it. The second half
of the question is where the real thinking shows — good answers usually come from outside rocketry.

---

### Prompt C — The motivation one

> **"Why this, and not one of the many other things you could be doing with your time at UVCE?"**

**Why we ask it:** we want to know whether they have actually thought about it. *"It sounds cool"* is
an honest and acceptable answer if they then explain what specifically interests them. *"I have always
loved space"* with nothing behind it is weaker.

---

### Prompt D — The builder's one

> **"Describe something you have built, fixed, made, or figured out. What went wrong, and what did you
> do about it?"**

**Why we ask it:** 🔴 **this is our favourite prompt.** It does not have to be a rocket, or even
technical. A repaired bicycle, a hacked phone, a reprogrammed calculator, a fixed fan — the subject
does not matter. **What matters is whether they ran into a problem, got stuck, and kept going.**

Anyone who has done that once will do it again.

---

## Marking guide (20 marks)

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| **Genuine thought** | 6 | Has an actual opinion, not a summary of the reader. |
| **Specificity** | 4 | Concrete examples, details, numbers, names — not vague generalities. |
| **Self-awareness or honesty** | 4 | Willing to admit uncertainty, weakness, or a mistake. |
| **Clarity of writing** | 3 | Readable. Does not need to be elegant. |
| **Length discipline** | 3 | Within 400–600 words. **Padding loses marks here, and so does ignoring the limit.** |

### What to reward

- Admitting they do not know something
- An example from their own life rather than a generic one
- A question they want answered
- Disagreeing with the reader, *with reasoning*
- Saying honestly that they found something boring

### What to mark down

- 🔴 **AI-generated text.** Watch for: flawless generic prose, no personal detail, no specific
  examples, em-dashes in unusual places, and a suspiciously balanced "on the one hand / on the other
  hand" structure. **Ask them to talk about it in the interview** — a person who wrote it can discuss
  it; a person who generated it cannot.
- Copied paragraphs from the reader
- Padding to reach the word count
- Whole essay with no example from their own experience

> **On AI:** do not treat it as a crime. Treat it as uninformative. The essay exists to tell us about
> *them*, and generated text tells us nothing. A short, clumsy, obviously human essay beats a polished
> synthetic one every time.

## Practical handling

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Format** | Plain text, Markdown, or a document. Whatever they are comfortable with. |
| **Submission** | One shared folder. Name files `Essay_<Name>.md` so they sort. |
| **Deadline** | Same deadline as the test. **Do not extend it for individuals** — meeting a stated deadline is itself part of the assessment. |
| **Marking** | Two people mark independently, then reconcile. See [`06_Interview_Framework.md`](<06_Interview_Framework.md>). |

---

## How it feeds the interview

Pull **one specific line** from each essay into the interview. Example:

> *"You wrote that you are worried you will lose interest when things fail. Our first motor test will
> probably fail. Tell me how you think you will handle that."*

This does two things: it proves you actually read their essay, and it turns the interview into a real
conversation instead of an interrogation.

## Combined scoring

| Component | Marks |
|---|---|
| Open-book test | 50 |
| Essay | 20 |
| Doubt-session engagement (marker's judgement) | 10 |
| **Total before interview** | **80** |

Use this to rank for interview slots. **But do not treat it as a cut-off** — see the note in
[`04_Open_Book_Test.md`](<04_Open_Book_Test.md>): a candidate who failed the safety judgement question
needs a conversation regardless of their total, and a candidate with a weak test but an outstanding
essay deserves an interview.
