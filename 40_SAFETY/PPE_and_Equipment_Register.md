# UVCE Rocketry Club — PPE and Safety Equipment Register

**Document:** `40_SAFETY/PPE_and_Equipment_Register.md`
**Parent document:** `40_SAFETY/Safety_Manual_Master.md`
**Owner:** Club Safety Officer
**Purpose:** Define exactly what safety equipment the club must own, in what quantity, for what
purpose, at what cost, and in what order of priority — and define exactly what must **never** be used.

> **No energetic activity takes place until the equipment for that activity is present, inspected, and
> signed off.** "We'll get it next week" is a NO-GO.

### 2.2 Hand protection

| Item | Qty | Purpose | Approx. cost (₹) | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Heat / fire-resistant gloves** (split-leather welding gauntlets or aramid/leather heat gloves, rated for contact with hot surfaces) | **2 pairs** | Handling hot casting vessels, moulds, coring rods, and the motor immediately after firing. Leather welders' gauntlets are the practical choice — cheap, widely available, and effective. | 300–900 /pair | **MUST** |
| **Nitrile gloves** (disposable, powder-free, **5–6 mil**, box of 100) | **1 box** | Chemical-resistant barrier for handling potassium nitrate, sorbitol, and propellant. Protects skin from oxidiser contact and from minor contamination. | 400–900 /box | **MUST** |

> ### ⚠️ Glove rules
> - **Nitrile, not latex, not vinyl.** Latex degrades quickly and provides poor resistance; vinyl is
>   permeable to many chemicals and offers almost no protection. **Nitrile is the correct choice.**
> - **Nitrile gloves are not heat protection.** They melt. Never use nitrile gloves to handle anything
>   hot, and never wear them near molten propellant — wear leather for that.
> - **Gloves must fit.** A loose glove catches on a coring rod or a vessel handle and pulls your hand
>   into the hazard. A tight glove you cannot remove quickly is a burn risk.
> - **Remove rings, watches, and bracelets before any propellant work.** A ring traps hot material
>   against skin and turns a minor splash into a serious burn. It is also a snag hazard.

### 2.3 Body protection

> ### 🔴 NO SYNTHETIC CLOTHING AROUND PROPELLANT
> Nylon, polyester, rayon, acrylic, and synthetic blends **melt** at low temperature and **fuse into
> skin** when exposed to flame or molten propellant. Molten polymer burns are substantially worse than
> equivalent flash burns, are much harder to treat, and routinely require skin grafting.
>
> **Wear cotton or flame-resistant (FR) treated fabric, full coverage.** This means:
> - **Full-length sleeves**, not rolled up
> - **Full-length trousers**, not shorts, not track pants with synthetic content
> - **Closed leather or cotton shoes**, not sandals, not floaters, not open sandals — molten
>   propellant dripping into an open shoe is a life-changing burn
> - **Cotton socks**, not synthetic
> - **No synthetic inner layers touching skin** — including synthetic vests, synthetic undershirts,
>   and fleece. A fleece jacket will melt onto your torso.
> - **Hair tied back**; no loose clothing, no dangling sleeves, no scarves, no loose jewellery

| **Cotton / FR clothing set** (full-sleeve cotton shirt or FR coverall, full-length cotton trousers) | **4 sets** | All-over body protection. A one-piece cotton or FR coverall is better than separates because it has no gap at the waist and nothing to snag. | 600–2,000 /set | **MUST** |

## 4. Fire extinguishers — correct types and correct use

> **Using the wrong extinguisher on the wrong fire can kill you.** Water on an electrical fire
> electrocutes the operator. Water on burning metal can explode. The wrong agent on a propellant fire
> can knock the flame down and let you walk into a re-ignition.

### 4.1 Fire classes

| Class | Fuel | In our work |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Solid combustibles — wood, paper, cloth, cardboard, rubbish | Workshop, packaging, the site itself, dry grass |
| **B** | Flammable liquids — solvents, adhesives, oils, waxes, molten propellant | Epoxy, acetone, alcohol, melted sorbitol/KNSB |
| **C** | Flammable gases | LPG cylinders (if the site has any) |
| **D** | Combustible metals — aluminium, magnesium, sodium | **Aluminium and magnesium powders if we ever use them**; aluminium-loaded propellants |
| **Electrical** (not a formal class in all standards) | Energised electrical equipment | Heaters, grinders, thermostats, the firing box |

### 4.2 Which extinguisher for which fire

| Fire | Correct agent | **Never use** |
|---|---|---|
| **Burning KNSB propellant (any quantity)** | **Large volumes of water**, or **dry sand** to smother | A small amount of water (spatters molten propellant); CO₂/dry powder as the *only* agent (knocks down flame without cooling — **re-ignition risk**) |
| **Small propellant fire on a bench** | Water, sand, fire blanket | — |
| **Molten propellant spill that has ignited** | **Smother with sand**; cool surrounding area with water | A directed water jet (spreads molten material) |
| **Electrical fire (heater, grinder, wiring)** | **CO₂** | **Water — electrocution risk.** Dry powder is acceptable but destroys the equipment |
| **Solvent / epoxy / oil fire** | **CO₂**, **dry powder**, or a **fire blanket** for a small pan fire | Water on an oil fire (spreads it violently) |
| **Wood / paper / grass fire** | **Water**, **ABC dry powder**, sand | — |
| **Burning aluminium or magnesium powder** | **Dry sand only.** Class D agent if available | **Water — can react violently and produce hydrogen. CO₂ — can react with hot metal. Dry powder — ineffective on Class D** |
| **Clothing on fire (a person)** | **Stop, drop, and roll. Wrap in the fire blanket.** Then cool with water | A fire extinguisher directed at a person's face. Never aim at the head — CO₂ and dry powder can asphyxiate |

### 4.3 Extinguisher placement — the rules

1. **Within 5 metres of the workstation**, in the direction **away** from the escape route — so that
   reaching for the extinguisher does not put you between the fire and your way out.
2. **Mounted, visible, unobstructed.** Not behind a stack of boxes, not under a bench, not in a
   cupboard.
3. **Pressure gauge in the green** — checked at every session and recorded.
4. **Service date current** — CO₂ and dry powder extinguishers require periodic professional servicing.
   An extinguisher that has been used, even partially, is **recharged or replaced immediately**.
5. **Everyone must have physically picked it up and squeezed the handle once** during training. An
   extinguisher you have never operated is an extinguisher you will fumble.
6. **Location marked.** At a field site, everyone must be able to point at the extinguisher without
   looking around.

### 4.4 Why CO₂ and dry powder are not the answer to a propellant fire

CO₂ and dry powder work by **excluding oxygen and interrupting the flame chemistry**. They do **not**
remove heat. A propellant grain or a pool of molten KNSB holds enough heat to reignite the moment the
blanket disperses and air returns. The classic failure mode is: operator discharges CO₂, flame goes
out, operator steps forward to inspect, propellant reignites.

**Water works because it removes heat — it is a cooling agent, not a smothering agent.** That is why
the dunk bucket and water buckets are mandatory and why "get more water" is the correct instinct for a
propellant fire, and why **water buckets must be large (≥ 20 L) rather than token.**

## 6. 🚫 DO NOT USE — prohibited items and practices

This section is as important as everything above it. Each item below has caused injuries in amateur
and professional rocketry.

### 6.1 Materials and clothing

| ❌ Never | Why |
|---|---|
| **Synthetic clothing (nylon, polyester, rayon, acrylic, fleece) near propellant** | **Melts and fuses into skin.** Molten polymer burns are far worse than flash burns and routinely require grafting. Cotton or FR only |
| **Sandals, floaters, open shoes, or bare feet** | Molten propellant dripping into open footwear causes severe, disabling burns |
| **Loose sleeves, scarves, dangling jewellery, untied hair** | Snag hazards around rotating machinery (grinders, tumblers, drills) and they catch molten material |
| **Rings and watches during propellant work** | Trap hot material against skin; snag hazards. Turn a splash into a serious burn |
| **Latex or vinyl gloves for chemical work** | Poor chemical resistance; latex degrades fast. **Use nitrile** |
| **Nitrile gloves for handling anything hot** | They melt. **Use leather for hot work** |
| **Contact lenses during casting or firing** | A hot fragment or splash under a lens is much worse than on the eye surface. **Wear glasses or goggles instead, and remove lenses on propellant days** |

### 6.2 Containers and tools

| ❌ Never | Why |
|---|---|
| **Glass containers for propellant, oxidiser, or molten material** | Shatters. Molten propellant plus broken glass plus your hands is the worst combination available |
| **Steel or ordinary iron tools near dry oxidiser or mixed powder** | **Steel on steel or steel on concrete sparks.** A spark in oxidiser dust is an ignition source. **Brass, beryllium-copper, wood, silicone, or plastic only** |
| **Metal utensils in a coated heating vessel** | Scratches the coating → hot spots → local overheating → potential ignition |
| **Improvised heating vessels** (open-element hotplate, electric coil stove, gas flame, microwave, induction hob with a thin pan) | **An exposed element or a flame is a direct ignition source.** Powder falling onto an element at several hundred degrees ignites immediately. **Thermostatically controlled, no exposed elements, only** |
| **Any vessel without a working thermostat** | Casting requires **120–130 °C, ±5 °C**. Uncontrolled heating has no upper bound. A pot on a gas flame can exceed 300 °C, which is the auto-ignition temperature |
| **Reused food containers, unlabelled containers, drink bottles** | Someone will eventually mistake it for food or drink. **Labelled, dedicated containers only** |
| **Plastic bags or cling film for storing propellant long-term** | Not airtight enough against humidity; not rigid enough for protection; and cling film is a static generator |
| **Vacuum cleaners for oxidiser or propellant spill** | Dust goes through the motor, the motor sparks, and the bag becomes a bomb. **Sweep gently with a soft brush and damp cloth; bag and remove** |

### 6.3 Practices

| ❌ Never | Why |
|---|---|
| **Grinding or milling mixed propellant** | The mixed powder is **readily combustible**. Friction and heat in a grinder can ignite it. **Oxidiser and fuel are ground and processed SEPARATELY, always** |
| **Grinding potassium nitrate that may contain fuel residue** | Same reason. Dedicated grinder for oxidiser only. Clean and inspect between batches |
| **Using one grinder for both oxidiser and fuel without full cleaning** | Cross-contamination creates a combustible mixture inside a machine that generates heat and sparks |
| **Water on an electrical fire** | Electrocution |
| **Water on burning aluminium or magnesium powder** | Violent reaction, hydrogen generation |
| **A small amount of water on molten propellant** | Spatters and spreads it. **Use large volumes, or sand** |
| **Directing an extinguisher at a person's face** | CO₂ and dry powder can asphyxiate. For clothing fires: stop, drop, roll, fire blanket |
| **Firing a motor without shunted igniter leads** | Stray current — induced, static, or from a wiring fault — can initiate the igniter. **Shunt until the moment of connection** |
| **Relying on the Safe/Arm switch alone** | **It is only PARTIAL security.** Layer it with shunting, physical power disconnection, a key held on the Test Director's person, and distance |
| **Working alone with propellant** | If you are burned you cannot help yourself. And alone, you will guess where you would otherwise check |
| **Re-entering a test site for equipment** | Equipment is replaceable. Wait for the Safety Officer's all-clear |
| **Standard "boilerplate" thick-wall motor cases** | Thick-walled cases store a great deal of energy before bursting and **increase** the severity of a failure. Size cases for a **burst pressure of no more than 2.5 × MEOP** (`Safety_Manual_Master.md` §3.4) |
| **A burst diaphragm to force pressure build-up** | Documented by Nakka as the **cause of a motor failure** (Flight C-18, July 1980). **Do not use one** |
| **"Improving" the KNSB formula without a formal research authorisation** | Even small additive changes dramatically alter burning characteristics and can cause catastrophic failure of a proven design |
| **Using perchlorates or chlorates** | For the experienced only. **This club does not have that experience and does not use them** |

## 7. Sourcing in India

### 7.1 Where to buy what

| Category | Best source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Safety glasses, chemical goggles, face shields** | Industrial safety shops; online marketplaces (Amazon.in, IndiaMART, Flipkart) | Buy **IS/EN 166 marked** products. Local industrial safety suppliers in Bengaluru (Peenya, Bommasandra, Rajajinagar industrial areas) stock these at better prices than online for bulk |
| **Nitrile gloves, dust masks, ear defenders** | Industrial safety suppliers; medical/pharmacy suppliers; online | Buy nitrile by the box, not the pair. Check the mil thickness — thin "exam" gloves tear |
| **Leather welding gloves, leather aprons, welding screens** | Welding supply shops; industrial safety suppliers | Welders' suppliers are the cheapest source and the products are built for heat |
| **FR / cotton coveralls** | Industrial workwear suppliers; online | Verify the FR certification if buying "FR". Ordinary thick cotton is an acceptable minimum |
| **Fire extinguishers (CO₂, ABC dry powder, water)** | **Licensed fire safety dealers only** | Extinguishers require **periodic professional servicing and refilling**. Buy from a dealer who will also service them, and keep the service record. Do not buy second-hand or unlabelled |
| **First aid supplies, burn dressings, eyewash** | Medical/pharmacy suppliers; hospital supply shops; online | **Check expiry dates on receipt.** Buy burn-specific dressings explicitly — a generic kit will not have them |
| **Non-sparking tools (brass scrapers, spatulas, scoops)** | Industrial tool suppliers; hardware stores (brass items); online | **Beryllium-copper is expensive and imported.** Brass is adequate for our scale and much cheaper. **Note: beryllium-copper dust is itself toxic — handle and clean with care** |
| **Grounding straps, grounding rods, copper cable** | Electrical supply shops; industrial electrical suppliers | **The wrist strap MUST have the integral 1 MΩ resistor.** Verify before buying |
| **Thermometers (IR and probe)** | Industrial instrument suppliers; online | Buy a **calibratable** IR thermometer with a stated accuracy. Cheap "kitchen" IR thermometers often have ±3–5 °C error, which eats your entire ±5 °C casting tolerance. **Buy two and cross-check** |
| **Thermostatically controlled deep fryer / skillet** | Home appliance shops; online | **Verify no exposed heating element** where powder could land. Verify the thermostat holds a setpoint stably. Test it with **water only** before ever putting propellant in it |
| **Stainless steel vessels, silicone spatulas, HDPE containers** | Restaurant supply shops; hardware; online | Dedicated to propellant work. **Never return them to food use** |
| **Sand, buckets, drums** | Local hardware stores; building material suppliers | Cheapest items on the list and among the most important. **Buy more buckets than you think you need** |
| **Potassium nitrate, sorbitol** | **NOT through ordinary retail channels.** Only via the authorised procurement route approved by the faculty advisor, with the purchase register maintained | **See `Safety_Manual_Master.md` §3.2.** Potassium nitrate is a restricted oxidiser under the Explosives Act 1884 / Explosives Rules 2008, administered by PESO. Do not improvise sourcing |
| **Load cells, pressure transducers, DAQ, cameras** | Electronics suppliers (SP Road, Bengaluru); online | **SP Road is the best local source for electronics in Bengaluru.** Buy spares for anything critical |

### 7.2 Sourcing rules

1. **Three quotes for anything over ₹5,000.** The club is spending members' and sponsors' money.
2. **Do not buy safety equipment on price alone.** The cheapest face shield may be 1 mm thick and
   distort your vision; the cheapest extinguisher may have no service network.
3. **Buy the correct specification, not an approximation.** "Splash goggles" and "safety glasses" are
   different products. "Nitrile" and "latex" are different products.
4. **Keep every invoice.** The club's BOM, its CDR estimate-vs-actual reporting, and any future
   compliance question all need a paper trail.
5. **Everything bought for a safety purpose is logged in this register** with its purchase date,
   service dates, and location. The register is a live document.
6. **Never accept donated safety equipment without inspecting it.** A donated fire extinguisher with an
   unknown service history is not equipment — it is a false sense of security.
7. **Budget for consumables annually** — nitrile gloves, dust masks, dressings, saline, burn gel, and
   extinguisher servicing are recurring costs, not one-time purchases.
