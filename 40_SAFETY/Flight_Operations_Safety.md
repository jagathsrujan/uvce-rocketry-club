# UVCE Rocketry Club — Flight Operations Safety

**Document:** `40_SAFETY/Flight_Operations_Safety.md`
**Parent document:** `40_SAFETY/Safety_Manual_Master.md`
**Applies to:** every model and experimental rocket flight conducted by the club, from an A-motor
low-power flight to any larger experimental vehicle

> **A rocket leaves your control the moment it leaves the pad.** You cannot steer it, you cannot stop
> it, and you cannot recall it. Everything that keeps a flight safe has to be decided **before** the
> countdown — the site, the angle, the wind, the clearance, and the recovery area. Once it is flying,
> you are a spectator of your own decisions.

---

## 1. Site selection

### 1.1 The fundamental requirement

> **You must be able to lose the rocket and the motor completely, and have nobody and nothing harmed.**

That is the acceptance test for a site. Not "it will probably be fine". Not "there's nobody around
today". **A site is suitable if the worst-case outcome — the rocket flying off at an unplanned angle,
the motor CATO-ing on the pad, the recovery system failing, the rocket landing under power — still
results in no injury and no damage to anyone else's property.**

If the honest answer to that is "no", the site is wrong.

### 1.2 Minimum site dimensions

| Motor class / expected apogee | Minimum clear area | Minimum distance to people, roads, buildings |
|---|---|---|
| **A–C motors, apogee < 150 m** | **150 m × 150 m** cleared | **50 m** |
| **D–E motors, apogee 150–400 m** | **300 m × 300 m** cleared | **100 m** |
| **F–G motors, apogee 400–800 m** | **500 m × 500 m** cleared, or a clear radius of **at least 1× the expected apogee** | **200 m** |
| **Above G class / experimental motors** | **Clear radius of at least 2× the expected apogee**, and reference the IN-SPACe advisory thresholds | **500 m minimum**, scaled with impulse |

> **The rule of thumb that overrides the table:** the **clear radius should be at least the expected
> apogee** for anything above D class, and **at least twice the apogee** for a first flight of a new
> design or a new recovery system. **A rocket that goes up 500 m can come down 500 m away — and further
> with wind.**

**Measure the site. Do not estimate it.** Walk or drive the dimensions before the first flight at any
new site. GPS the corners. Write the dimensions down.

### 1.3 What the site must NOT contain

| ❌ | Why |
|---|---|
| **Dry grass, crop stubble, or dry brush in summer** | **The single biggest fire risk in Indian model rocketry.** A rocket landing in dry grass with a hot motor casing, or a CATO on the pad, starts a fire that spreads faster than you can run. **In summer, a green or bare site only** |
| **Roads, highways, railway lines** | Vehicles cannot react. A rocket landing on a highway causes a crash |
| **Overhead power lines** | A recovery system or a rocket body contacting a line is an electrocution risk to whoever retrieves it, and can cause a line fault |
| **Buildings, huts, sheds, farmland with workers** | Third-party property and third-party people |
| **Airports, airstrips, helipads, drone corridors, military areas** | **Legally and practically off limits.** See §9.3 |
| **Villages, temples, schools, markets, public gathering areas** | Crowds cannot be controlled and will not stay behind a line |
| **Livestock grazing areas** | Animals cannot be briefed and will investigate the pad |
| **Water bodies used by people** | A recovery in water is a drowning risk |
| **Prohibited or restricted areas, forest reserves, protected land** | Legal exposure and no permission possible |
| **Public footpaths and trails crossing the field** | You cannot guarantee clearance. Someone will walk into the range |
| **Sites where you cannot control access** | If you cannot stop people walking in, you cannot fly |

### 1.4 Vegetation and fire

1. **In summer (roughly February–May in Karnataka), fly only from a green, mown, bare, or wetted
   site.** Dry grass is tinder.
2. **Clear the pad area of vegetation for a radius of at least 3 m**, and wet the ground around the pad
   before a launch.
3. **Have a water source at the pad** — a minimum of **two 20 L buckets** — plus a **correct fire
   extinguisher within 5 m** of the pad.
4. **Have a fire watch** — a person whose only job is to watch for fire, with the water and the
   extinguisher immediately to hand.
5. **If a fire starts, it is the priority, not the rocket.** Abandon recovery and fight the fire from
   the upwind side. Call 101 if it spreads beyond immediate control.
6. **After every launch, inspect the landing area for smoke or smouldering material** before leaving.
   A hot motor casing in dry grass can ignite a fire **minutes after landing**.
7. **Never fly on a Red Flag / high fire-danger day.** If the local forest or fire department has
   issued a fire-danger warning, the launch is cancelled.

### 1.5 Landowner permission — written, not verbal

> **Get written landowner permission on paper, with a validity date.** This is a requirement of the
> IN-SPACe advisory checklist, and it is the club's credibility when approaching any official.

**The permission letter must contain:**

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Landowner's full name and signature** | Not a verbal "yeah, go ahead" from a caretaker |
| **Contact number and address** | So the club can notify and so officials can verify |
| **Survey number / plot identification** | So there is no ambiguity about which land |
| **The exact activity permitted** | "Model rocket launches, up to X class motors, on the following dates" — not "some experiments" |
| **The date range of validity** | With an expiry date. Renew it annually |
| **The specific dates or the process for notifying dates** | Advance notice in writing for each launch day, where required |
| **The club's undertaking** | That the club will leave the land as found, compensate for any damage, and control access |
| **The Safety Officer's and faculty advisor's contact details** | So the landowner has someone to call |
| **A statement about access** | Which gate, which track, where vehicles may park, where they may not drive |
| **Acknowledgement of fire risk and the club's mitigation** | Particularly important in summer |

**Carry a copy on site at every launch.** Photograph it, and store it with the club's permanent records.

### 1.6 Site re-verification

**Before every launch day, Range Safety re-checks:**

- [ ] Nobody has started living, working, or grazing on the land
- [ ] No new construction, excavation, or overhead line has appeared (or has been reported to the club)
- [ ] No harvesting activity (which brings workers and machinery into the field)
- [ ] Vegetation condition — is it still green and mown, or has it dried out since permission was obtained?
- [ ] Access control still works — can the club still stop people entering?
- [ ] The recovery area is still clear of people, crops, and obstacles
- [ ] The landowner's permission is still valid, and the landowner has been notified of this launch day

## 2. Launch angle and safety

### 2.1 The core rule

> **Avoid vertical launches wherever possible.**

**Why:** a vertical launch has **no safe downrange direction and no safe uprange direction.** Every
direction is equally likely to receive the rocket — and every direction contains something. A rocket
that goes straight up and comes straight down lands on the pad or next to it — which is where the
people are. A rocket that weathercocks, or that suffers a rod-exit instability, can go anywhere.

**An angled launch puts the entire flight path, and the entire impact area, in one known direction,
into a known clear area.** That is the whole point: it converts an unpredictable hazard into a
predictable one.

### 2.2 Launch angle rules

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Angle from vertical** | **Launch the rod or rail tilted AWAY from the flight line** — 5° to 15° from vertical for model rockets, into the clear area |
| **The competitor / competition configuration** | **80°–85° from horizontal** (i.e. 5°–10° from vertical), lockable and verifiable — this is the IN-SPACe Model Rocketry Competition requirement (rule 5.iv). The pad's angle is checked by the Jury before launch |
| **Never launch into the wind beyond the recovery area** | Angle **away from** the spectators and the flight line, **toward** the clear recovery area |
| **Never launch toward** | People, roads, buildings, livestock, power lines, dry vegetation, or anything you cannot replace |
| **Low-angle launches** | Below 60° from horizontal, **the entire trajectory is inside the danger envelope** — the rocket flies low and far, and the impact area is enormous. **Not for model rockets without a proper range** |
| **Do not exceed the pad's design angle** | A pad that cannot be locked at the set angle will move. **The angle must be locked and verified** |

### 2.3 The 90-degree (vertical) case — safe range in ALL directions

**If you do launch vertically — for example, a competition flight specified at 80–85°, or a
certification flight requiring vertical flight — then there is no "safe direction", and therefore:**

> **The safety radius must be all-round, and it must be equal to the maximum expected altitude plus a
> margin.**

| Expected apogee | All-round minimum clear radius for a vertical launch |
|---|---|
| 100 m | **150 m** |
| 200 m | **300 m** |
| 400 m | **600 m** |
| 800 m | **1200 m** |
| 1000 m | **1500 m**, and the full IN-SPACe advisory process applies |

**This is why most club flying should be angled.** A vertical 200 m flight needs a 300 m all-round
clear radius — which very few accessible sites near Bengaluru have. An angled 200 m flight into a
300 m clear sector is far easier to arrange, far easier to control, and far easier to explain to a
landowner.

### 2.4 Pad setup

1. **The pad is set back at least 10 m from the firing line** for a model rocket, and further as motor
   class increases. **Nobody stands at the pad during launch.**
2. **The pad must be stable** — staked, weighted, or bolted. A pad that tips moves the launch angle.
3. **The rod or rail must be long enough** for the rocket to reach stable flight velocity before it
   leaves the rod. **A rocket that leaves a short rod too slowly will weathercock or tumble.**
4. **The rod or rail must be clean and straight.** A bent rod changes the launch angle and can impart
   a tumble.
5. **The angle must be locked and marked**, and the same angle used for every flight of the day unless
   deliberately changed and re-briefed.
6. **The pad must be free of dry vegetation for 3 m around**, and the immediate ground wetted in summer.
7. **Electrical continuity:** the pad's launch leads are **shunted** until the moment of connection, and
   the ignition box is at the firing line with the operator. **The same rules as `Static_Test_Procedure.md`
   §4 apply: shunted leads, key switch, Safe/Arm in series with FIRE, continuity check under 50 mA, and
   the recognition that Safe/Arm is only partial security.**
8. **The pad is the most dangerous place on the range.** Once the rocket is on the pad and the igniter
   is installed, **nobody goes to the pad except the person arming it, and only while the system is
   SAFE with the key out and the leads shunted.**

## 3. Wind limits and pre-launch weather assessment

### 3.1 Why wind is the dominant weather hazard

- **Wind tilts the trajectory.** A rocket flying at 30 m/s in a 8 m/s crosswind drifts substantially
  downwind — potentially out of the intended recovery area and into something you did not plan for.
- **Wind affects the recovery.** A parachute drifts with the wind all the way down. A 5 m/s wind over a
  60-second descent carries a rocket 300 m downwind of the landing point it would have had in still air.
- **Wind destabilises the launch.** A rocket on a rod in a gust can be pushed off the rod before it has
  flying speed, and then it **weathercocks into the wind** — a classic low-altitude, high-speed,
  unpredictable flight.
- **Wind makes recovery hard.** A rocket in a tall tree or on a roof is a retrieval problem; a rocket
  in a tree next to power lines is a safety problem.

### 3.2 Wind limits

| Wind speed (sustained, at 2 m height) | Action |
|---|---|
| **0–15 km/h** | **GO.** Ideal |
| **15–25 km/h** | **GO with caution.** Re-check the recovery area; expect significant drift; consider reducing the launch angle's downwind reach |
| **25–32 km/h** | **CAUTION.** Only with a proven design, a large clear site, and Range Safety's explicit go. **No first flights, no new designs, no untested recovery systems** |
| **Above 32 km/h** | **NO-GO.** Cancel or wait |
| **Gusts more than 10 km/h above the sustained speed** | **NO-GO** regardless of the sustained value. Gusts are what tip a rocket off the rod |
| **Any wind that will carry the rocket outside the permitted site** | **NO-GO**, regardless of the measured speed |

**Wind speed is measured, not estimated.** Use a handheld anemometer, or the nearest reliable weather
station reading. "It doesn't feel too windy" is not a measurement and is not accepted.

**Direction matters as much as speed.** Record the wind direction and **confirm that the drift is into
the clear area, not out of it.** A 12 km/h wind blowing toward a road is a NO-GO; the same wind blowing
into an empty field is fine.

### 3.3 Pre-launch weather assessment — the checklist

Completed by Range Safety **at the site, within 30 minutes of the first launch**, and re-checked before
each subsequent flight and whenever conditions change.

```
PRE-LAUNCH WEATHER ASSESSMENT          Date: __________  Time: ______
Site: ________________________________  Assessed by (Range Safety): ____________

[ ] Wind speed, sustained (2 m) ......................... ______ km/h   Limit: <= 32
[ ] Wind speed, gust .................................... ______ km/h   Limit: <= 42 (sustained+10)
[ ] Wind direction ...................................... ______ deg / from ____________
[ ] Drift direction is INTO the clear recovery area ..... Yes / No
    If No -> NO-GO
[ ] Estimated drift over descent (wind x descent time) .. ______ m
[ ] Predicted landing point remains inside the site ..... Yes / No
    If No -> NO-GO

[ ] Visibility: pad and recovery area clearly visible ... Yes / No
[ ] Cloud base / ceiling ................................ ______ m
    Ceiling must exceed expected apogee by >= 2x
[ ] Precipitation: none ................................. Verified
    Any rain -> NO-GO

    Above 38 °C -> reassess personnel heat stress, consider postponing

```

### 3.4 The go/no-go authority on weather

**Range Safety owns the weather decision, and it is a hard decision.** If the wind rises above the
limit mid-session, **launching stops.** Not "one more". Not "this one's already on the pad".

**Taking a rocket off the pad because the wind rose costs 5 minutes.** Launching it costs whatever the
wind decides.

## 4. Man-limits and material-limits

This is the conceptual frame that makes range safety work. Learn it and apply it to every launch.

### 4.1 The two limits

> ### MAN-LIMITS
> **No person shall be placed at risk. There is no acceptable number of casualties. A launch is
> prohibited if any person could be harmed — including people who have not consented, who do not know
> a launch is happening, or who have wandered into the area.**
>
> **The man-limit is absolute and it is zero.**

> ### MATERIAL-LIMITS
> **There is a limit to the property damage that is acceptable, and it is expressed in advance.**
> Losing the rocket is acceptable — that is a cost the club has budgeted. Damaging the landowner's
> fence, crop, vehicle, or building is **not** acceptable, and neither is creating a liability or a
> fire.
>
> **The material-limit is defined before the launch**, in the site assessment, and expressed as: *what
> is the worst thing this rocket can hit, and is that acceptable?*

### 4.2 How to apply them

**Method — done once per site, reviewed per flight:**

1. **Draw the range.** On a map or a sketch, mark the pad, the launch angle, the wind direction, and
   the **impact probability cone** — the sector the rocket could plausibly land in, including the
   downwind drift and the worst-case recovery failure.
2. **List everything inside that cone.** Roads, buildings, animals, people, crops, power lines, water,
   vehicles, footpaths.
3. **Apply the man-limit:** if **any person** could be inside the cone — now or during the flight — the
   launch is **prohibited**. Not mitigated. Prohibited.
4. **Apply the material-limit:** for everything else in the cone, decide **now**, before launching,
   whether the club is prepared to pay for it. If the answer is no, move the pad, change the angle,
   wait for the wind to change, or do not launch.
5. **Write the conclusions down** on the site sheet: *"Cone contains: empty field, one fence line at
   400 m. Man-limit: clear. Material-limit: fence is at the outer edge; accept."*
6. **Re-verify before every flight.** A tractor entering the field at flight 3 changes the assessment.

### 4.3 The failure modes you must plan for

| Failure mode | Where it goes | Implication for the cone |
|---|---|---|
| **CATO on the pad** | Fragments radially, motor hardware in all directions | **The firing line and spectators must be outside the pad fragment radius.** This is why the pad is ≥ 10 m from the line, and more for larger motors |
| **Rod-exit instability / weathercocking** | Rocket turns into the wind and flies low and fast, unpredictably | **The entire low-angle sector is a risk. This is the most common serious flight failure** |
| **Premature ejection at high speed** | Vehicle breaks up; parts fall from altitude | The whole cone, plus the area under the ascent path |
| **No ejection / lawn dart** | Rocket comes in ballistic and fast, and can penetrate | **A ballistic impact is lethal.** The man-limit is the only protection |
| **Parachute failure / tangled chute** | High descent rate | Same |
| **Long drift under chute** | Downwind, potentially far outside the planned area | **This is why the wind assessment and the downwind check matter most** |
| **Motor failure mid-burn** | Unpredictable, low | Whole cone |

> **The lawn-dart case is the one that decides the site.** A rocket that fails to deploy comes in
> ballistic at high speed and will go through a roof, a vehicle, or a person. **There is no mitigation
> for that other than a site where the impact area is empty.** If the impact area is not empty, do not
> fly.

## 5. Firing line discipline, countdown protocol, and the Safety Officer

### 5.1 Firing line discipline

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| **One launch control point** | One ignition box, one person operating it. **The launch control officer is the only person who presses the button** |
| **Everyone behind the firing line** | The line is a physical line — a rope, a row of stakes, a painted line. **Behind it, always, from before arming until the rocket has landed and the range is declared clear** |
| **Nobody at the pad** | Once the rocket is on the pad and the igniter is installed, **the pad is empty.** Nobody "just checks the clips" |
| **Minimum separation** | **Spectators at least 10 m behind the firing line** for model rockets, and further for larger motors. **The pad is at least 10 m in front of the line** |
| **Count every person** | The Safety Officer counts heads before each arming, and **accounts for every person by name** after each flight. **"I thought he was with you" is how people get hurt** |
| **Distance from the pad** | As motor class rises, the firing line moves back. **Never fewer than 10 m for a model rocket; use the static test distances (75–100 m) for anything experimental** |
| **Quiet at the countdown** | From T−10, **everyone stops talking**, except the person calling the count. **You must be able to hear "ABORT" and "STOP"** |
| **No phones in hand** | During arming, countdown, and flight. **Photography is by the designated documentarian only**, from a briefed position behind the line |
| **Ear and eye protection** | **Eye protection for everyone.** Ear defenders for anyone near the pad or for larger motors |
| **No smoking, no lighters, no open flame** | Anywhere on the range, at any time |
| **Children and animals** | **Not on the range.** Not behind the line, not "holding the button", not "just watching from close" |
| **Vehicles** | Parked **behind the firing line** and **out of the impact cone**, with windows up, for larger motors |
| **A physical barrier is better than a rule** | Rope, tape, cones, stakes. A rule requires compliance; a rope requires effort to break |

### 5.2 Countdown protocol

**Called by the Launch Control Officer, aloud, with responses from Range Safety.**

| Time | Call | Expected response / action |
|---|---|---|
| **T−5 min** | "FIVE MINUTES TO LAUNCH. RANGE STATUS?" | Range Safety: "RANGE CLEAR" / "RANGE NOT CLEAR" |
| **T−3 min** | "THREE MINUTES. PAD IS ARMED?" | Arming officer confirms the igniter is installed, leads shunted |
| **T−2 min** | "TWO MINUTES. WEATHER CHECK." | Range Safety reports wind speed, direction, gusts. **Any limit exceeded = HOLD** |
| **T−1 min** | "ONE MINUTE. ALL PERSONNEL BEHIND THE LINE. HEADS COUNTED." | Safety Officer confirms the head count by name |
| **T−30 s** | "THIRTY SECONDS. CAMERAS RUNNING. SKY CLEAR." | Documentarian confirms recording |
| **T−20 s** | "TWENTY SECONDS. ARMING." | Launch Control Officer removes the shunt and connects the leads. **Announces: "LEADS CONNECTED, SHUNT REMOVED"** |
| **T−15 s** | "FIFTEEN SECONDS. SYSTEM ARMED." | Arm switch to ARM. **Second person confirms the ARMED indicator** |
| **T−10** | **"TEN"** | Silence from everyone. **Nobody moves** |
| **T−9 to T−6** | "NINE… EIGHT… SEVEN… SIX…" | — |
| **T−5** | **"FIVE — RANGE CLEAR"** | Range Safety confirms once more |
| **T−4, T−3, T−2** | "FOUR… THREE… TWO…" | — |
| **T−1** | **"ONE"** | — |
| **T−0** | **"LAUNCH"** — or **"IGNITION"** | **Press and hold the launch button** |

**Abort calls:** **"ABORT"** or **"HOLD"** — anyone may call it, at any time, for any reason. On
hearing it: **stop the count, switch to SAFE, remove the key, disconnect the battery, announce "SYSTEM
SAFE" — and hold.** Nobody approaches the pad until the Safety Officer authorises it and the wait in
§7 has elapsed.

**There is no penalty for calling an abort.** There is a penalty for not calling one
(`Safety_Manual_Master.md` §4.4).

### 5.3 The designated Safety Officer at a launch

**One person, named for the day, distinct from the Launch Control Officer wherever club size allows.**
If the roles must be combined, that combination is **recorded explicitly** in the day's plan.

**Duties:**

- **Owns the head count.** Counts and names every person before arming, and accounts for every person
  after every flight. **This is the most important duty on the range.**
- **Owns the man-limit.** Confirms, before every flight, that nobody is in the impact cone. **Walks or
  drives the downrange area for the first flight of the day, and after any break in activity.**
- **Owns access control.** Nobody enters the range without the Safety Officer's knowledge.
- **Owns the pad area.** Confirms nobody is at the pad before arming, and physically checks after each
  flight.
- **Owns the fire watch** and confirms water and the extinguisher are at the pad and at the line.
- **Owns PPE compliance** for everyone on the range.
- **Owns the weather watch** jointly with Range Safety.
- **Has the unconditional authority to stop any launch, at any time, without justification.**
- **Leads the recovery** and the post-flight inspection.
- **Records the incident/near-miss log.**

**The Safety Officer does not launch rockets.** Their hands are free and their attention is on the
range, not on the countdown.

### 5.4 Roles for a small club — minimum viable

| Role | Minimum requirement |
|---|---|
| **Safety Officer** | **Always filled.** One named person. Does not launch |
| **Launch Control Officer** | One person, at the box, with the key on their person |
| **Range Safety / spotter** | May be combined with the Safety Officer only for the smallest flights, and only if recorded |
| **Documentarian** | Optional, but if present, briefed and behind the line |
| **Everyone else** | Behind the line, counted, quiet from T−10 |

**Nobody launches alone.** Ever. Even a single A-motor flight in a field requires **two people** — one
to launch and one to watch, count, and call an emergency. This is the same two-person rule as
propellant work.

## 6. Recovery procedures, identification, and marking

### 6.1 Recovery planning — before launch

1. **Determine the predicted landing point** from the launch angle, the wind, and the expected descent
   time. **Write it down.**
2. **Confirm the predicted landing point is inside the site**, inside the permitted area, and clear of
   hazards.
3. **Identify the retrieval route** — the safest walking or driving route to the landing area, and one
   that does not cross the range while anyone is still armed.
4. **Brief the recovery team**: who goes, which route, what they carry (water, first aid, gloves, a
   radio or phone), and when they go.
5. **Nobody moves from the firing line until the Safety Officer declares the range clear** — which
   requires the rocket to have landed, the system to be SAFE, the key removed, and the battery
   disconnected.

### 6.2 Recovery discipline

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| **The system is made safe before anyone moves** | Switch to SAFE, remove the key, disconnect the battery, announce "SYSTEM SAFE". A rocket that landed with the system armed still has a live ignition circuit |
| **Wait before approaching** | See §7. **A landed rocket may still have a live ejection charge** |
| **Approach from the side, not from the front** | The nose cone and any deployment mechanism point forward. Approach from behind or from the side |
| **Wear gloves** | A motor casing can be **hot enough to burn** minutes after landing, and the propellant residue is a chemical hazard |
| **Do not touch the motor until it is cool** | Hot hardware can reignite propellant when air reaches it |
| **Do not pull, shake, or pry a rocket** | It may contain a live igniter, a live ejection charge, or unburned propellant |
| **If the rocket is in a tree, on a roof, or on a structure** | **Do not climb.** Evaluate. If it cannot be reached safely, it stays there, and the club arranges safe recovery or accepts the loss. **No member climbs a tree, a pole, a roof, or a wall for a rocket** |
| **If the rocket is near or on power lines** | **Do not approach within 10 m.** Do not use a pole, a stick, or a ladder. **Contact the electricity utility.** A rocket near a line is a lost rocket. This is not negotiable |
| **If the rocket landed in water** | Do not enter the water. Use a line, a hook, or a boat. **Drowning is a real risk in Indian field conditions** |
| **If the rocket landed on a road** | Only retrieve when the road is clearly clear of traffic, and only with a spotter. **Never step onto a live carriageway** |
| **If a member of the public picks up the rocket** | Approach calmly and politely. Explain. **Do not grab it from them.** If it may contain live pyrotechnics, warn them clearly and take it only when it is safe. **No argument is worth an injury** |
| **If the rocket landed on private land outside the permission** | Do not trespass. Contact the landowner, explain, and ask permission. **If refused, the rocket is lost** |
| **Recovery is tracked** | Record where each rocket was found, and whether the recovery system worked as designed. **A recovery anomaly is data** |

### 6.3 Rocket identification and marking

> **Mandatory for every rocket flown by the club.** An unmarked rocket that lands on someone's land is
> an unidentified object that will be treated as suspicious, and a rocket that lands somewhere you
> cannot see is a rocket nobody can return.

**Every rocket carries, on the exterior, in a durable and legible form:**

| Marking | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Club name** | "UVCE ROCKETRY CLUB" |
| **Club identification number** | A unique number per airframe, correlated to the club's flight log |
| **Contact telephone number** | A number that is **answered** on launch days. An unanswered number is worse than no number |
| **Contact email address** | For follow-up |
| **"IF FOUND, PLEASE CALL — REWARD"** (optional but effective) | Encourages return rather than disposal |
| **Language** | **English, Kannada, and Hindi.** For flights in another state, the regional language of that state. *(The IN-SPACe Model Rocketry Competition rules require English, Hindi, and the regional language of the launch state — rule on markings. The club applies this standard to all its flying)* |

**Marking method:**

- **Permanent marker or paint** on the airframe, **plus** a **printed label** with a clear protective
  covering, **plus** a **marking inside** the airframe (so it survives exterior damage).
- **High-visibility colour** — fluorescent pink, red, or orange is the competition requirement and is
  also the practical choice. **A rocket you can see is a rocket you can follow and recover.**
- **Reflective tape** on the airframe and parachute improves visual tracking and recovery.
- **The parachute is also marked** with the club name and number — parachutes drift furthest and are
  the most commonly lost component.

### 6.4 Visual tracking

1. **Designate a spotter** whose only job is to follow the rocket with their eyes from launch to
   landing. **The spotter does not launch, does not film, and does not take photographs.**
2. **Spotters look at the pad, not at the sky.** Follow the rocket from the ground up, not from the sky
   down — you will not find it in the sky.
3. **Note the cardinal direction and an estimated distance** at apogee, at ejection, and at landing.
4. **Have binoculars available** where the flight is expected to go high.
5. **If a rocket is lost from sight, mark the last known direction immediately** and search along that
   line before wandering.
6. **Two spotters are better than one.** Eyes get tired and people look away.

## 7. Misfire and hang-fire procedure on the pad

> **A rocket sitting on a pad with an igniter installed is a loaded device.** It may fire at any
> moment. It may fire because of a stray current, a static discharge, a delayed pyrotechnic reaction, or
> a slow-burning igniter. **It does not care that you want to go home.**

### 7.1 Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Misfire** | The launch command was given and **nothing happened**. No ignition, no smoke, no sound |
| **Hang-fire** | The launch command was given and there was **some** response — smoke, a spark, a fizz, a partial ignition — but the rocket did not lift or lifted weakly |
| **Partial ignition** | The motor lit, burned weakly, and did not produce enough thrust to lift the rocket, or lifted it only a short distance |

**A hang-fire is more dangerous than a misfire**, because it proves the ignition circuit works and that
something is burning. Something that is burning can finish burning at any moment.

### 7.2 Misfire procedure

**Phase 1 — Make safe, without moving (immediately)**

1. **Continue to hold the launch button** for 5 seconds past the expected ignition time. Do not release
   and re-press.
2. If nothing has happened after **5 seconds**, call **"MISFIRE"** — loudly, so everyone hears.
3. **Release the button.**
4. **Switch to SAFE.**
5. **Remove the key** and put it in your pocket.
6. **Disconnect the firing-circuit battery** at the box.
7. **Announce: "SYSTEM SAFE. HOLDING. NOBODY MOVES. NOBODY APPROACHES THE PAD."**
8. **Everyone stays behind the firing line.** Nobody walks to the pad to "have a look".

**Phase 2 — Wait**

| Situation | Minimum wait before approach |
|---|---|
| **Clean misfire** — no smoke, no sound, no heat, no visible response | **60 seconds minimum** for a model rocket, **5 minutes** for anything experimental or above D class |
| **Hang-fire** — smoke, spark, fizz, partial burn, or any response at all | **5 minutes minimum** for a model rocket, **15 minutes** for anything experimental |
| **Partial ignition / the motor burned but did not lift** | **15 minutes**, and the vehicle is treated as a live energetic device |
| **Anything unexplained** | **30 minutes**, and the Safety Officer decides. **When in doubt, wait longer — then wait again** |

**Why so long for a hang-fire?** A burning igniter, a smouldering delay element, or a heat-soaked
propellant can complete its burn late. **A rocket that launches while your hand is on it is the worst
possible outcome**, and it is entirely preventable by waiting.

**Phase 3 — Approach (only after the wait, only with the Safety Officer)**

1. **The Safety Officer authorises the approach**, out loud, after confirming the wait has elapsed.
2. **Two persons minimum.** Safety Officer leads.
3. **Approach from the side**, never from in front of the nose or directly behind the nozzle.
4. **Walk. Do not run.** Watch for debris.
5. **DISCONNECT THE IGNITER LEADS FIRST — before touching anything else.**
   - Disconnect the launch leads at the **box end** first if the run allows.
   - **Then disconnect the igniter from the launch leads.**
   - **Then, and only then, touch the rocket.**
   - **Immediately SHUNT the igniter leads** (twist the bared ends together).
   - **Never tug on the igniter wires.**
6. **Feel for heat** before touching the motor. If it is warm, **back off and keep waiting.**
7. **Remove the rocket from the pad only when the system is safe, the igniter is disconnected and
   shunted, and the motor is cool.**
8. **Record everything** before disturbing it: igniter condition, whether the bridgewire burned, motor
   temperature, elapsed time, weather at the time.

### 7.3 Hang-fire — the additional rules

1. **Treat the vehicle as a live energetic device** until the motor is confirmed cold and the igniter is
   removed.
2. **Do not attempt to remove a partially burned motor from the vehicle by force.**
3. **The motor is retired** after a hang-fire or partial ignition. **It is not re-flown.** A motor that
   has partially burned has unknown internal geometry.
4. **The vehicle is inspected for damage** — heat damage to the airframe, the recovery system, the
   avionics, and the fins — before it flies again.
5. **The cause is investigated on the bench, not at the pad.** Two consecutive misfires on the same
   ignition system = the system is retired pending investigation.

### 7.4 Misfire and hang-fire prohibitions

| ❌ Never | Why |
|---|---|
| **Approach the pad before the wait has elapsed** | A late ignition with your hand on the rocket |
| **Send someone to "just check the clips"** | The wait applies to everyone equally |
| **Touch the igniter before disconnecting the leads** | Disconnect first. Always |
| **Re-press the launch button after a misfire, "just once more"** | If the circuit is live and the igniter is in the motor, you may be arming a delayed event. **The system is made SAFE first, the cause is understood second, and only then is a re-attempt considered** |
| **Re-attempt without removing the rocket from the pad and re-shunting** | The full arming sequence is repeated from the start. **A re-attempt is a new launch, not a continuation** |
| **Reuse the igniter** | Replace it. Never "top it up" with more powder |
| **Re-fly a motor that hang-fired** | Unknown internal geometry |
| **Assume the rocket is safe because nothing is happening** | **Time and a disconnected, shunted circuit** are the criteria. Not appearance |
| **Skip logging it** | Misfires are data and warnings |

## 8. Range clearance and downrange checks

### 8.1 Range clearance — the verbal protocol

> **Only the Safety Officer (or the designated Range Safety) may declare the range clear, and the
> declaration is made out loud, every time, before every arming.**

| Call | Meaning | Who says it |
|---|---|---|
| **"RANGE IS CLEAR"** | I have physically verified that nobody and nothing is at the pad, in the exclusion zone, or in the impact cone, and it is safe to arm | Safety Officer / Range Safety |
| **"RANGE IS NOT CLEAR"** | Someone or something is in the range, or I cannot verify that it is clear. **Arming does not proceed** | Anyone |
| **"ABORT" / "HOLD"** | Stop the count immediately | **Anyone** |
| **"SYSTEM SAFE"** | Arm switch to SAFE, key removed, battery disconnected | Launch Control Officer |
| **"ALL CLEAR"** | The flight is complete, the rocket has landed, the system is safe, and the range may be re-entered | Safety Officer |

**"RANGE IS CLEAR" is never assumed, delegated to silence, or inferred from the fact that nobody has
said otherwise.** If Range Safety has not said it, **the range is not clear**, and the launch does not
proceed.

### 8.2 Downrange check

**Purpose:** confirm that the rocket's entire possible impact area contains no people, no animals, and
nothing the club is unwilling to damage.

**Method:**

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Determine the impact cone** from the launch angle, wind direction and speed, expected apogee, and the descent time. **Include a generous margin — a 50% wider angle than the nominal trajectory** |
| 2 | **Walk or drive the cone** for the **first flight of the day**, and after any break in activity longer than 30 minutes, and after any change in wind |
| 3 | **Look for:** people, workers, herders, children, animals, vehicles, tractors, machinery, footpaths, cyclists, picnic groups, fishermen, and anything new since the last check |
| 4 | **Check the sky** — aircraft, drones, birds, kites. **Kite strings are a real hazard in Indian fields and can be invisible** |
| 5 | **Confirm with the landowner or a local** whether anyone is expected in the field |
| 6 | **Report back to the firing line** with an explicit "CLEAR" or "NOT CLEAR", and the direction and distance of anything found |
| 7 | **Record the check** in the day's log, with the time and the person who did it |

**For a vertical launch, the cone is all-round.** There is no shortcut: the entire circle at the radius
of §2.3 must be verified clear.

### 8.3 Access control during flight operations

| Measure | Detail |
|---|---|
| **A single controlled entry** | A gate, a gap in a hedge, a track. **Range Safety knows who is on the site and can account for every person** |
| **Signs at the entrances** | "ROCKET LAUNCHING IN PROGRESS — DO NOT ENTER — DANGER" in **English and Kannada**, with the club's phone number |
| **A person posted** | If the site cannot be physically closed, **post someone at the access point** for the duration. A person is more effective than a sign |
| **Vehicles off the range** | Parked behind the firing line and outside the impact cone |
| **Late arrivals** | **No entry during a countdown.** Wait for the "ALL CLEAR", then be briefed by the Safety Officer before entering |
| **Anyone who leaves** | Reported to the Safety Officer, so the head count stays accurate |
| **Children and animals** | **Not admitted to the range at all.** No exceptions |

### 8.4 Post-flight actions

1. **Announce "ALL CLEAR"** once the rocket has landed, the system is SAFE, the key is out, and the
   battery is disconnected.
2. **Count heads again.** Every person accounted for by name.
3. **Inspect the pad area** for fire, hot debris, and unburned propellant. **In summer, this is
   critical** — a hot casing in dry grass ignites a fire minutes later.
4. **Conduct the recovery** per §6.2.
5. **Debrief the flight** while it is fresh: did the motor perform as expected? Did ejection occur at
   the right time? Did the recovery system work? Any anomaly? Any near miss?
6. **Log the flight** in the club flight log: rocket ID, motor, date, site, weather, angle, apogee
   (estimated or measured), recovery outcome, and any anomaly.
7. **Before leaving the site:** the range is walked and cleared of all debris, unfired igniters, and
   litter. **Everything the club brought is carried out.**
8. **Any incident or near miss** is logged per `Safety_Manual_Master.md` §8.

## 9. Working with local administration and the landowner

### 9.1 Why this section exists

Amateur rocketry in India operates in a framework that most members have never had to think about. The
club's ability to keep flying depends on **being legitimate, being transparent, and being known to the
people whose permission and forbearance the club relies on.**

**A club that is a known, polite, organised presence gets cooperation. A club that appears
unannounced with a rocket gets reported, shut down, and possibly investigated.**

### 9.2 The landowner

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Written permission** | Per §1.5. **Before the first launch, not after** |
| **Advance notice for each launch day** | A phone call the day before, and a message on the morning of. **Do not simply turn up** |
| **Explain what you are doing** | In plain language, in the local language where possible. Show them the rocket. Explain the size and the noise. **People fear what they do not understand, and a frightened landowner withdraws permission** |
| **Ask about their concerns** | Crops, livestock, workers in the field, a festival, a family event, recent rain. **Their concerns are the site's constraints** |
| **Respect the land completely** | No driving off the track. No litter. No damage to fences or crops. **Leave it exactly as found, or better** |
| **Compensate immediately for any damage** | A broken fence, a damaged crop, a rutted track. **Pay for it the same day, without argument.** Your reputation on that land is worth more than the money |
| **Give them a contact** | A number that is answered. Tell them to call if they find debris, or if anyone asks questions |
| **Follow up after each launch** | A short message: "the launch went well, thank you, we cleared the site". **This is the single most effective way to keep permission** |
| **Renew the permission in writing annually** | With a new validity date |
| **If the landowner says no, it is no** | No persuasion, no pressure, no launching anyway. **Find another site** |

### 9.3 Local administration and official notification

**The club's obligations depend on the scale of what is being flown. The following is awareness, not
legal advice — the Safety Officer confirms the current position with the faculty advisor before every
launch campaign.**

**IN-SPACe advisory guidelines on sub-orbital launches and model rocketry** set out a graduated
approach, as recorded in the club roadmap:

| Total impulse | Indicative requirement |
|---|---|
| **Below 10,000 N·s** (covers essentially all model rocketry and the club's current work) | **Inform the local administration** |
| **10,000 – 40,960 N·s** | **30 days' prior intimation plus advisory** |
| **Above 40,960 N·s (≈ O-class)** | **IN-SPACe authorisation required** |

**What "inform the local administration" means in practice for this club:**

1. **The faculty advisor is the channel.** Official communication goes through the faculty advisor, on
   institutional letterhead, **not** from individual students.
2. **Notify before the launch campaign, not on the day.** A written intimation to the local police
   station and/or the district administration, describing: who the club is, what it is flying, where,
   when, the maximum altitude, the motor class, the landowner's permission, and the club's safety
   arrangements and contact details.
3. **Carry copies on site.** The permission letter, the intimation, the test/flight plan, and the
   safety procedures.
4. **Be prepared for a visit.** If a police officer or an official arrives, **be polite, stop launching
   if asked, and produce the paperwork.** Nobody argues with an official on the range. The faculty
   advisor is called.
5. **Never fly near or toward a sensitive installation.** Defence establishments, airports, airbases,
   ports, dams, and power infrastructure are absolute no-go areas, at any scale. **If you can see it,
   fly somewhere else.**

### 9.4 Airspace awareness

Even a small rocket can conflict with aircraft and drones. There is no formal airspace clearance
process available to a student club for low-power flight, so the club's protection is **site
selection and observation**:

- **Never fly within 5 km of an airport, airstrip, helipad, or a marked drone corridor.** Identify
  these **before** choosing a site, not after.
- **Look up before every launch.** Aircraft, helicopters, drones, and birds. **If anything is in the
  sky in the impact cone, do not launch.**
- **Kites are a real and serious hazard** in Indian open land, and kite strings are nearly invisible.
  **If kites are flying anywhere in the area, do not launch.**
- **For anything above the model rocket scale**, airspace and range coordination is a matter for
  IN-SPACe and the designated range, not for the club to arrange itself.

### 9.5 Working with the public and the curious

- **Be welcoming and be clear.** "We're a college rocket club, we're launching small rockets, it's
  safe, but please stay behind this line."
- **Never allow a member of the public onto the range**, however enthusiastic, however senior, however
  insistent.
- **If someone is unhappy**, stop launching, engage politely, and call the faculty advisor. **Do not
  escalate.**
- **If someone picks up the rocket**, see §6.2.
- **If someone is filming or photographing**, that is fine — from behind the line, after a briefing.
- **Every interaction with a stranger is a test of whether this club gets to keep flying.** Treat it
  that way.

## 11. NO-GO criteria for flight operations

**Any single item below disqualifies the flight. There is no vote and no override.**

1. **Any person, animal, or vehicle in the impact cone or the exclusion zone**
2. **A member of the public who has not been briefed** within the minimum distance
3. **The landowner's permission is not on site, has expired, or has been withdrawn**
4. **The landowner has not been notified for today**
5. **Any person at the pad after the igniter is installed**
6. **The head count is not complete** — someone is unaccounted for
7. **Wind above limits** — sustained > 32 km/h, gusts > 10 km/h above sustained, or any wind that
   carries the rocket outside the permitted site
8. **Wind direction carrying the drift outside the clear area**
9. **Lightning within 10 km**, thunder heard in the last 30 minutes, or a thunderstorm approaching
10. **Any rain, or wet ground at the pad**
11. **Cloud ceiling below 2× the expected apogee**
12. **Less than 90 minutes of daylight** remaining
13. **Local fire-danger warning in force**, or dry vegetation in the impact area in summer without a
    fire watch and extra water
14. **No fire cover** at the pad — no water, no extinguisher, no fire watch
15. **No first aid kit, or no burn dressings, or no eyewash**
16. **Aircraft, helicopter, drone, kite, or large bird flock** in the sky over the area
17. **Within 5 km of an airport, airstrip, helipad, or drone corridor**
18. **Sight of a defence installation, dam, port, or major power infrastructure**
19. **Igniter leads not shunted**, or the launching circuit not verified
20. **Ignition box key not on the Launch Control Officer's person**
21. **Continuity check not performed**, or performed in a way that could deliver firing current
22. **Battery voltage low** or unverified
23. **Rocket unmarked** — no club name, ID, or contact number
24. **Rocket damaged** — cracked fin, deformed body, damaged nose cone, degraded recovery system
25. **Recovery system not packed, not verified, or of untested design on a first flight**
26. **Launch angle not set, not locked, or not verified**
27. **Pad unstable**, or the rod/rail bent or too short for the vehicle
28. **Firing line not established**, not marked, or people in front of it
29. **Any person on site without eye protection**
30. **Any person on site who has not been briefed** on the range rules, countdown, and abort calls
31. **Phones in hand** during arming, countdown, or flight
32. **Any person unfit for duty** — fatigued, ill, impaired
33. **Fewer than two people present.** **Nobody launches alone**
34. **A member has raised a safety concern that has not been resolved to their satisfaction**
35. **Any unexplained anomaly** — a component that does not look right, a measurement that does not
    match, a change nobody can account for
36. **Schedule pressure being used as a reason to proceed**

> **Item 36 causes all the others.** A rushed launch is a launch with no downrange check, a guessed
> wind, an un-shunted igniter, and a rocket nobody counted. **If you are rushing, stop.**
